### VB-404: where the elite comes from (raised 12 November 2026)
From the balance review of 12 November (`docs/balance/findings.md`; `docs/balance/cards.md`, card 1). Four mock models tested slice 5's road from debt to land at the food ratio the game has (1.64 to 1.86 times need, under 1% of households hungry): at most one elite household in 150 years, rent and interest at 6 to 10% of output, land won by hard lenders, and nothing until the map is full (about year 150). Office and a tithe gave 12 households (77 people) by year 150 with no rise in land concentration, and nothing opposes them. The road from hunger through gifts and goodwill to land moved 70 fields in 250 years. The numbers come from mocks that mix the game's rules with invented ones.
1. Which roads should carry the 75? **P:** three, in the order they open. First, office: a household gains standing by feasts and good-year gifts (VB-407), standing elects it to the alderman's office (VB-314), and the office has an income (question 3). This is your road from goodwill to prestige, given a destination. Second, debt and land as slice 5 designs it, kept as the late and slow road once the map is full, with its ceiling of one field a hearing. Third, rank from outside (slice 8). The band for the wrap-up names the share each carries.
2. The class rule. Rents and stores at 1.5 times need, three year ends running, counted 45 to 100 of 200 households as landed in the mock with no rents at all. **P:** landed when what a household receives from others (rents, an office's income, fees, interest, owed labour at 3 rations a day) has covered at least 0.75 of its need at three year ends running, and no longer after three below 0.5; its own harvest and stores do not count.
3. What an office earns. A tithe is a tenth of everything every field produces, and overfed the elite in the budget (57% over its need with no tenancy). **P:** before any Church, the alderman keeps a share of what households give the village store each year as the office's keep, a quarter to start with, and is fed at the village's feasts; the Church's tithe comes with slice 8 and goes to the Church. The plan sets the share so that one office keeps one household at about 1.5 times need in a village of 15 farms.
4. Patronage (agreed on 12 November: where kin help falls short a rich household gives, and the goodwill is worked off as days on its farm and weighed in the debtor's vote). As worded it was negligible in the mock: the owed work is cleared in a year or two. **P:** make it a lasting tie: while a household owes a patron more than 30 days of its need, it gives 2 days at the patron's harvest each year and its holder leans to the patron's side in a vote; the tie ends when the balance decays or is repaid, so goodwill's halving in 4 years is its brake.
5. The brakes. Debtors' politics was the steepest brake in the mock (the remedy turning to labour once debtors hold 30% of the voice left 15 estates per 200 households; at 20%, 6.6); tenants leaving did not brake at all; fully partible inheritance cut estates and pauperised. **P:** keep debtors' politics (VB-320) and one field a hearing as the ceiling; drop "tenants leave" from the list of brakes; treat inheritance as elite turnover, not as a brake, with "buys out the rest" the default.
6. Do the refactors VB-310 (Obligations), VB-311 (own and use) and VB-313 (Decisions) go ahead before this is answered? **P:** yes: every road above needs obligations, rights held apart and the meeting's questions; VB-312 and VB-314 to VB-321 wait for this plan.

### VB-405: marriage, nursing and growth (raised 12 November 2026)
From the balance review of 12 November (`docs/balance/findings.md`; `docs/balance/cards.md`, card 2). Measured on populous small: growth 1.2 to 2.2% a year, almost nobody never marries, women marry at 20 to 21, and marriages put off for want of a home rose from none to 52 to 122 a decade. A cohort mock with the game's numbers gives a ceiling of 2.4% a year; 10 to 1,000 in 150 years needs 3.1%; founders reach about 1%.
1. How should marriage answer to scarcity? Today the ages are fixed (18 and 20) and a home must hold 1.5 years of food above its seed (`MARRIAGE.means`). **P:** keep the gate and treat its means test as the valve's setpoint, tuned so that the food ratio late in the game sits where the target needs it; add nothing that sets an age. The age then rises by itself as homes grow scarce, as it already does. Add the second half of the historical valve through service: a servant does not marry while in service, so a village short of land sends more of its young into service and more of them marry late or never.
2. An infant's death and the nursing gap. Today a mother nurses for 48 days whether the child lives or not. **P:** the child's death ends it. It replaces losses (worth about half a point of annual growth in the mock) and is the part of nursing that regulates itself; the length of nursing stays fixed until a custom of it is wanted.
3. How does an island reach 1,000? **P:** by settling families, stated as a number: one family of another culture every 2 years until 300 are living, then one every 10; and founders arrive as four unrelated families, so the kin limit does not bar the first marriages. In the mock a family every 2 years reached about 1,000 at year 150, and the same for 60 years only reached about 850. This is more than "very limited" early and matches it late.
4. Is 150 years to 1,000 the pace wanted? **P:** 150 to 200 game years, reported as a band; the vision's 40 to 80 hours of play holds either way at the speeds the page offers.
5. Which mix of brakes should hold 1,000? Every mix has a cost: by hunger alone about half of children die; by marriage at 18 alone about half never marry; marriage at 26 with a quarter never marrying costs the fewest lives; a town at 3.3 times the farms' death rate drops life expectancy to 23. **P:** late marriage and service first, the town's deaths second (at about 1.5 times the farms'), hunger a rare event of bad years, and disease designed for the town only.
6. Should a balance held by marriage be allowed to overshoot? In the mock it overshot by 3.5 to 4% sixty to seventy years after a famine and echoed for a generation. **P:** yes: it is a visible, historical wave of a generation, small against the seed noise of 20%; it is reported in the band and not damped.

### VB-407: feasts and keeping (raised 12 November 2026)
From the balance review of 12 November (`docs/balance/cards.md`, cards 3 and 4). You agreed that feasting and good-year gifts should drive the goodwill economy, and suggested longer keeping as a late-game lever. About a fifth of the grain harvest rots today.
1. When does a household hold a feast? **P:** at an occasion (a wedding, a funeral ale, the harvest home once a year) when its stores hold more than 2 years of its need above the seed; it spends up to a quarter of what it holds beyond those 2 years, as ale and food.
2. Whom does it ask, and what do they owe? **P:** its kin and the households its holder has the strongest ties to, up to 20 guests (ale's number); each guest household is booked goodwill to the host's credit for the customary worth of what its members ate and drank, by the gift's rule, so the feast's worth returns as help in a bad year. Standing stays ale's rule (0.05 a barrel for 3 years, at most 4).
3. May a household give in a good year without being asked? **P:** yes, to kin first: on its planning day after the harvest a holder with the same surplus gives a household it has a tie to and that holds under a year of need, up to its allowance; generosity (the holder's own reserve, a stand-in until traits) decides how often.
4. Keeping. **P:** nothing changes until facilities (slice 6): a granary then halves `ECON.storeLoss` for the household that builds one, and the plan names the side effect (larger stores, more to lend). Salting and drying stay as built.
5. Animals as stored wealth. **P:** a herd counts as an outlet of the surplus (fodder and hay into an animal that keeps), and is designed with the customs of the animals (VB-141), not here.

### VB-283: a household nobody can come to (raised 10 November 2026)
VB-283 replayed the ten full runs of the bands (`docs/bench/lone-households.js`; tables in `backlog/VB-283.md`). At `38b920d`, after VB-282 (a hungry household may eat its seed), 56 of the 225 household-years hungry over 0.3 after year 3 are of households with at most one grown member (34 households; 35 rows hold one field). The orchestrator decided, for you to overturn, that such a household is taken in when its seasonal ask for help falls short: a free relative moves in, else a servant (VB-308, ready; `design.md`, Households short of people, Taken in before it starves). That reaches 14 or 15 of the 56. In 42, nobody could come; 33 of those had a parent's, child's or sibling's household within reach. What becomes of them moves a Decided point, so it waits for you (VB-309, blocked).
1. Who holds the farm when its people go to kin? **P:** the kin household holds it in trust: it works the fields and takes the harvest, the owner and the holder stay the same, the house stands empty and decays; the trust ends when the holder marries or takes a servant at a moving round (they move back), when a ward comes of age, or at the holder's death (succession as today). The farm stays the family's, as the task asks, and the relation is the one splitting land already has (`design.md`, Handover and shares: the old household works the sibling's fields, Agreed 30 Oct), so VB-034 can reuse it. The other way: the farm is given up and stands empty, held by the village, with the family's birthright to claim it back (the machinery of a farm that dies out; nothing new, but the owner changes and the land goes out of use).
2. The orphan rule (Decided, 1 Nov: with no relative to be guardian, the children go to kin and the farm stands empty). **P:** the kin who take the children hold the farm in trust in the same way until the heir is 18, so the farm stays the family's. The other way: keep the rule as decided; VB-309 then serves lone adults only.
