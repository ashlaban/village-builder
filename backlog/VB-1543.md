Acceptance criteria:
- [x] design.md's Water row (Goods and the farming year) is rewritten from the decisions below, with what is Decided and what Proposed marked, and its change log line.
- [x] Access: what counts as fresh water (river, lake and stream tiles; which the map has), how the walk from the house is measured, and the access a site has, shown before a house is placed (the site search, and the played leader's choice among sites).
- [x] The fetch: the job's length as the walk to the nearest source or well and back, at or near zero beside fresh water or with a well of one's own; how it is ranked so that it is done (VB-626 and VB-1542's findings), and whether a dry day remains and what it costs.
- [x] The thirsty pawn: what thirst is on a pawn (a need of its own or the household's dry days read by the pawn), at what level a pawn leaves its work to drink or fetch, and how that is a verb any pawn has.
- [x] A well's two costs, kept apart in the design and in its numbers. Its chore cost: the daily fetch from it, the walk from the house to the well, near zero for a well of the farm's own and the walk to the green for a village's. Its construction cost: the hours of digging, once, flat a well and rising with the depth to the water (the depth read from the ground: soil and height above the nearest water, or a field of the map), independent of who or how many will draw from it.
- [x] Wells are a household's and a village's, both projects: what a village well saves a farm founded beside it (a lower chore cost from the first day, with no digging of its own), what a household gains by digging its own (the chore cost that is left), and what today's `village.well` becomes.
- [x] The fetch grows with the household. Two thresholds are set, each in people: how many one carrying by hand covers a day, and how many a cart covers; above the first the choice is more trips or the cart, and the daily cost at a site is given for a household of 1, at each threshold and above the second.
- [x] The waterside is attractive and scarce, not penalised: the design says how few land tiles lie a step from a river against the footprint a farm with its fields takes, counted on the maps (tiles within one step of fresh water, and how many farms they hold), so that the cost of the waterside is that it is taken.
- [x] The tasks that build it are opened, with the estimate of cost (households x one fetch a day x a path lookup) named before building.

## Notes
- Kim, 23 November. A chore alone is not interesting: what matters is fresh water access, and second what share of the daily plan the fetching takes. The decision is the player's when placing the farm: how much of the daily chore budget the site is worth.
- Decided by Kim: walked, not charged (the job stays, its length the real walk), and of high priority; a pawn who is really thirsty sees to it. A farm by a river or fresh water has the access of one with a well. Wells are a household's and a village's: a house costs less to found in a village with a well, and a household frees more time by digging its own. A well's construction cost is flat a well and rises with the depth to the water; its chore cost, the daily fetch from it, is a separate thing (Kim, 23 November: do not conflate them).
- Kim, 23 November, on the open points. The fetch grows with the household, with two thresholds that matter: how many people carrying by hand covers, and how many a cart covers. Rivers are to be attractive, as in Sweden, where the rivers were the more easily settled; what limits them is land: few land tiles lie a step from a river, and a farm with its fields takes a large footprint. Freezing is not considered for now.
