# Medieval towns and crafts, 900–1500

27 November 2026 · research for Kim. Copied from the Claude Doc of the same name
(https://claude.ai/artifact/DciAaL3Lzgyp1DqiVJbdwH), with Kim's answers to its questions added at the end. The research
is a source for design, not design: what the game takes from it is in `design.md`, section 17, Game mechanics.

Medieval Sweden was a land of farms with a handful of small towns: Stockholm, the largest, had about 5,000–8,000 people
around 1450, against Lübeck's 25,000. Farms made most of what they used; towns held the specialist crafts, the market and
the link to Hanseatic trade. Peasants had to sell in town, and what they mainly bought there was salt, iron and cloth.

- **Layout:** long narrow plots along one or two shore streets, alleys down to the water, a market square and church;
  walls only after c. 1250 (section 2).
- **People:** burghers (a third of Stockholm's taxpayers German), craftsmen's households, servants from the countryside,
  clergy, seasonal foreign guests (section 3).
- **Food:** peasant market sales, archipelago fish and firewood by boat, rents in kind, and the townspeople's own cows,
  pigs and gardens (section 4).
- **Crafts:** textiles, wood, leather and simple iron on every farm; shoemakers, tailors, smiths, coopers, bakers and
  masons in town; craft guilds rare outside Stockholm before 1500 (section 5).
- **Trade:** farm to town, coast to town, town to Lübeck; iron, copper, butter and furs out, salt, cloth, wine and beer
  in (section 8).

## 1. Chronology and town types

Sweden had very few towns, and they were small: Stockholm, the largest, held about 5,000–8,000 people in the late Middle
Ages, and most other Swedish towns 1,000–2,000 at most ([Gustafsson 2024][gus]; [Stockholm during the Middle Ages][sth]).
For comparison, Lübeck had about 25,000 and Cologne about 40,000–50,000 in the 15th century. Over 90% of Swedes lived on
farms throughout the period (approximate, from general syntheses).

| Phase | Typical place | What it was | Nordic examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| c. 750–975: emporia | Seasonal or semi-permanent trading site on a shore, under a king's protection | Craft and exchange hub; not yet a legal town | Birka (Mälaren), Kaupang, Ribe, Hedeby |
| c. 975–1200: royal and church towns | Planned plots along one main street, a royal estate, a mint, many churches | Seat of power and the new Church; magnates kept town plots | Sigtuna (c. 980), Lund (c. 990), Skara, Old Uppsala nearby |
| c. 1200–1500: chartered burgher towns | Harbour town with a market place, a council and a written town law | Self-governing burghers with a monopoly on retail trade; German merchants prominent | Visby, Stockholm (c. 1250), Kalmar, Söderköping, Lödöse, Turku |

On the Continent the same shift ran earlier and denser: Roman-era cities (Cologne) revived, and from the 12th century
hundreds of new towns were founded on planned grids, Lübeck (1143/1159) being the model for the Baltic. Swedish town law
was unified by King Magnus Eriksson's town law (*Magnus Erikssons stadslag*) around 1350; Stockholm had earlier used the
*Bjärköarätt* ([Gustafsson 2024][gus]).

## 2. Town structure and layout

The basic unit was the long, narrow plot (*tomt* or *gård*) with its short side on a street and its long side running back
to a yard or the shore. Sigtuna's plots were about 7–8 m wide and 30–40 m long, every house facing the main street, Stora
gatan; the town was laid out to a plan around 980 ([Viking Ship Museum, Sigtuna][sig]; excavations by Sten Tesch).

| Element | Nordic form | Continental form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan | One or two long streets along the shore, alleys (*gränder*) running down to the water | Planned grid or radial plan around a market (Lübeck, East German foundations) |
| Plot | Long narrow plot; street house in front, workshops, byres and storehouses around a back yard | Same idea, often deeper; burgage plots taxed by street frontage |
| Centre | Market square (Stockholm: Stortorget), parish church, later a town hall | Market square, town hall with cloth hall, parish churches, friaries |
| Harbour | Beach landing, then wooden quays built out into the water, filling the shore as land | Quays, cranes, warehouses on the river or harbour front |
| Defence | Often none until 1250+; Stockholm walls first mentioned 1288; Visby ring wall c. 1300 | Walls and gates the norm from the 12th–13th c. |
| Fringe | Gardens, cabbage yards, grazing commons, the town's own fields (*stadsjord*) | Suburbs outside the gates, gardens, vineyards, town commons |

Stockholm shows the Nordic pattern well. It had no regular grid and grew piecemeal on the island of Stadsholmen. The two
oldest streets are Köpmangatan ("merchants' street") and Skomakargatan ("shoemakers' street"); seagoing ships moored at the
Cog Harbour (Koggahamnen); bulk goods were sold at Kornhamnstorg ("grain harbour square") and Järntorget ("iron square");
fishermen and carpenters lived on the eastern waterfront ([Stockholm during the Middle Ages][sth], citing Nilsson and Rye
2002 and Hall 1999). Street names record where crafts clustered.

## 3. Who lived in towns

A town was a crowd of households, not of individuals: a master, his wife, children, journeymen, apprentices and servants
under one roof. In Stockholm about one third of taxpayers were German (mostly from Lübeck, Danzig and Westphalia), and many
others came from Finland ([Stockholm during the Middle Ages][sth]). Most newcomers, though, were young people from the
surrounding countryside.

| Group | Status | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burghers (*borgare*) | Sworn members of the town; only they could keep shop and sell retail | Merchants and master craftsmen; had to own property in town ([Gustafsson 2024][gus]) |
| Council (*råd*) and mayors | Elite merchants elected from the burghers | Magnus Eriksson's town law split seats evenly between Swedes and Germans; the German share was abolished in 1471 |
| Craft workers | Journeymen, apprentices; women in brewing, baking, selling | Stockholm's 1460 tax roll: 43 carpenters, 40 fishermen, 25 shoemakers, 24 tailors, 17 master masons, 8 bakers, 8 brewers, 7 butchers |
| Servants and labourers | Unpropertied, often rural migrants | Porters, carters, dock workers; the largest group, poorly recorded |
| Clergy | Under church law | Parish priests, friars (Franciscans, Dominicans), nuns, hospital staff |
| Guests (*gäster*) | Visiting merchants, mostly from across the Baltic | Could stay only from spring to 8 September; wholesale only; a local host answered for them ([Gustafsson 2024][gus]) |
| Royal and noble households | Outside town law | Castle garrison (Stockholm's castle on the north of the island); nobles kept town houses |

Towns grew only through migration. Death rates were higher than in the countryside, and plague struck often: the Black
Death probably reached Stockholm in late summer 1350, with repeated outbreaks after (Myrdal, cited in
[Stockholm during the Middle Ages][sth]). For a farming village this means towns pulled younger sons and daughters away as
servants.

## 4. Feeding the town

Towns were fed mainly by peasants who sold surplus at the town market, by rents and tithes in kind flowing to the Crown,
Church and nobles who lived there, and by the townspeople's own animals and gardens. Imported grain mattered only in the
largest ports and in Norway. Medieval Swedish law forced this flow into towns: buying and selling in the countryside
(*landsköp*) was banned, so peasants had to bring goods to a town market.

| Channel | What came in | Who controlled it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market days | Grain, butter, cheese, meat, hides, eggs, firewood, hay | Burghers set prices and weights; town officials inspected |
| Peasant seafaring (*bondeseglation*) | Salted and dried fish (Baltic herring, *strömming*), firewood, hay, stone, sand | Coastal peasants (Roslagen, the archipelago) allowed to sail their own and neighbours' goods to Stockholm ([Sjöfartsmuseet guide][sjo]; its dating is loose) |
| Rents, taxes and tithes in kind | Grain, butter, fish, iron, livestock | Crown castles, bishops, monasteries; sold on or eaten by their households |
| Urban farming | Cows, pigs, hens on the plot; cabbage, beans, peas, hops in yards; town fields and commons | Every household that could; town herd on common grazing |
| Long-distance imports | Salt (essential for preserving), grain into Norway, wine, beer, spices | Merchants, many of them German ([Gustafsson 2024][gus]) |

Archaeology supports both sides. Broad beans were grown in 13th-century Bergen and on the edges of Trondheim and Oslo
([Museum of Cultural History, Oslo][khm]). Large bone collections from Bergen and Trondheim are being re-studied for cattle
husbandry and urban diet, and recent work argues that fish and other marine food have been underrated in that diet
([University of Bergen][uib]). Ingvild Øye's work on Norwegian towns stresses town commons and close ties to farms around
them.

For Skärgård the main point: an archipelago farm near a town sells fish, firewood, hay and butter, and buys salt, iron
goods, cloth and beer. Salt is the bottleneck good, because without it the fish cannot be kept through winter.

## 5. Crafts in town and in the countryside

Most making happened on farms, by the household, in the slack seasons; towns held the crafts that needed a steady market,
costly raw materials or a fixed kiln, forge or workshop. The divide was about scale and selling, not skill: a farm wove its
own cloth, but a town tailor cut imported cloth for paying customers.

| Craft | On the farm (rural) | In town |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Textiles | Every household spun and wove wool and flax, mostly by women; coarse woollen cloth (*vadmal*) was even used to pay taxes | Tailors cut imported fine cloth; Stockholm's tailors got the oldest surviving Swedish guild privilege, 1356 |
| Iron | Peasants in Bergslagen smelted bog and rock ore in bloomeries (*osmund* iron); village smiths made tools and nails | Specialist smiths, locksmiths, armourers; Birka made padlocks at a scale never found on rural sites (Callmer, in Hansen, Ashby and Baug 2015) |
| Wood | Building, boats, tools, tubs; tar burning | Coopers (barrels for fish, butter, beer), carpenters (43 in Stockholm in 1460) |
| Leather | Tanning for own shoes and harness | Shoemakers (25 in Stockholm in 1460), saddlers, glovers |
| Food and drink | Baking, brewing, butter, cheese for the household | Bakers, brewers, butchers selling daily; ale and bread under price rules |
| Antler, bone, metal casting | Rare | Comb makers and bronze casters in Birka, Sigtuna, Ribe, Kaupang, often working several crafts ([McGraw and Mjærum 2024][kau]) |
| Stone and brick | Field-stone foundations | Masons and brickmakers for churches, walls, town houses |

**How crafts were organised.** In the Viking Age, scholars debate whether urban craftsmen were mobile specialists
travelling between markets or settled townspeople ([Ashby][ash]). By the later Middle Ages a town craft was a household
workshop: one master, a few journeymen and apprentices, the wife running sales and accounts.

**Guilds.** Two kinds existed. Religious fraternities (*gillen*), such as the guilds of St Gertrude or the Holy Body in
Stockholm, mixed members and held feasts, masses and care for the sick and dead. Craft guilds (*skrån*, *ämbeten*)
regulated a trade: who could be a master, prices, quality and the number of journeymen. In Sweden craft guilds were mostly a
Stockholm matter before 1500, and their written ordinances describe an ideal more than daily practice (Lindström; see
[Scandia][sca]). On the Continent craft guilds were far stronger: in Cologne they took a share of town government in 1396.

## 6. Housing and daily life

Until about 1300 Nordic towns were built almost wholly of timber, in the same log or post techniques as farms; brick and
stone came later, first for churches and rich merchants. By the late 15th century most of central Stockholm was brick
([Stockholm during the Middle Ages][sth]), while smaller Swedish towns stayed mostly wooden into the 1600s.

| Who | House | On the plot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rich merchant (Visby, Stockholm) | Tall stone or brick house, gable to the street; cellar, warehouse lofts above, living rooms in between | Yard, kitchen house, privy, sometimes a stable |
| Master craftsman | One- or two-storey timber or brick house; workshop and sales counter at street level | Workshop sheds, store, a pig or cow, well |
| Labourer, widow, servant | Rented room or small cottage at the back of someone else's plot, or in an alley | Shared yard and privy |
| Fisherman, carpenter (Stockholm's east shore) | Small timber houses near the water | Boat landing, drying racks |

Life on a plot looked much like a farmyard squeezed into 8 m of width: animals, middens, wells, latrine pits and workshops
side by side. Waterlogged town layers (Bergen, Trondheim, Oslo, Sigtuna, Lund) preserve this in detail. Fire was the great
risk: whole districts burnt repeatedly, and town laws ordered fire watches and buckets.

## 7. Social life

Town social life ran through four institutions: the council and its court, the parish and the friaries, the guilds and
fraternities, and the market. Each had a fixed calendar.

| Institution | What happened | Calendar hook |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Council and town court (*rådstugan*) | Disputes, debts, fines, burgher oaths; Stockholm's court books survive from the 1470s | Stockholm's ordinances (*bursprak*, preserved from 1459) were read aloud twice a year; the town law once each summer in the main square ([Gustafsson 2024][gus]) |
| Church | Mass, baptisms, burials, saints' days, processions; friars preached and ran hospitals | Christmas, Easter, Corpus Christi, the parish saint's day |
| Guilds and fraternities | Feasts (*gillesöl*), masses for dead members, help for the sick and widows | Annual guild feast; Stockholm's guilds of St Gertrude and the Holy Body drew merchants and craftsmen alike |
| Markets and fairs | Weekly markets; big fairs drew peasants and foreigners | Uppsala's *Disting* market in February; the Skåne herring market, August to October, the largest in the North |
| Taverns and bathhouses | Ale houses, the town wine cellar (the only legal place to sell wine in Stockholm), public bathhouses | Saturdays and feast eves |

Towns also had sharp divides. Germans and Swedes competed for council power; in 1389 a German faction killed Swedish
burghers in the Käpplinge murders ([Stockholm during the Middle Ages][sth]). Widows often kept a master's workshop running,
and women brewed, baked and sold at market, though few appear in tax rolls by profession (13 named in Stockholm in 1460).

For peasants, the town was a place visited a few times a year: for a market day, a fair, a church feast, or to pay a debt
or answer in court.

## 8. Trade: local, regional, Hanseatic

Trade ran on three nested scales, and a farm touched all three only through the town: peasants sold to burghers, burghers
sold to German merchants, and those merchants shipped to Lübeck and beyond. The law protected this chain by banning rural
trade and limiting what guests could do.

| Scale | Who traded | Goods out of Sweden | Goods in | Rules |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Local (farm to town) | Peasants, burgher shopkeepers | Fish, butter, grain, firewood, hay, hides, livestock | Salt, iron tools, cloth, beer, pots | Sell only in towns (*landsköp* banned); town weights and measures |
| Regional (coast, Mälaren, Gotland) | Coastal peasants sailing their own boats, small skippers, burghers | Fish, timber, tar, lime, stone | Grain, salt, finished goods | *Bondeseglation* tolerated or licensed for some coasts; Gotland peasants traded overseas until Visby pushed them out (civil war of 1288) |
| Long-distance (Hanseatic) | German merchants and Stockholm's big burghers | Iron (*osmund*), copper (Falun), butter, furs, hides | Salt, cloth, wine, beer, spices, luxury goods | Guests wholesale only, from spring to 8 September; wine only via the town cellar; salt and bulk at street stalls ([Gustafsson 2024][gus]) |

**The Hansa** was a loose league of north German merchant towns led by Lübeck, at its height from about 1350 to 1450, with
offices (*Kontore*) in Novgorod, Bergen, London and Bruges ([Britannica][bri]). Visby was its first Baltic hub and
flourished in the early 14th century; the Danish king Valdemar IV took it in 1361, and Stockholm and Danzig grew in its
place. In Sweden, German merchants dominated export trade ([The Hanseatic World and Scandinavia][uva]).

**Payment.** Coins circulated from the 990s (Sigtuna's mint) but much trade was credit, barter and payment in kind; taxes
were long paid in butter, grain, *vadmal* and iron. Common units were the barrel (*tunna*) and the ship-pound (*skeppund*).

## 9. What this suggested for Skärgård

The historical shape is a farm that makes almost everything itself and goes to town a few times a year to swap surplus for
the few things it cannot make. The mechanics this suggests are carried into `design.md`, section 17, Game mechanics, as
Proposed.

| Historical fact | Possible mechanic |
| --- | --- |
| Farms did textiles, wood, leather and simple smithing in slack seasons | Winter and bad-weather tasks are crafts at home; no workshop building needed for basic goods |
| Towns held crafts needing markets, kilns or imported materials | Fine goods (iron tools, good cloth, barrels, pots) come from town, or from a later specialist building |
| Salt, iron and cloth were the main buys | A short import list with salt as the bottleneck for keeping fish and meat |
| Fish, butter, firewood and hay were the archipelago's sales | The village's export goods, taken in by boat |
| Rural trade was banned; only towns could buy | One trading partner (the town) with set prices and a market calendar |
| Market days, fairs and feasts were fixed in the year | A seasonal calendar of town visits (for example a February market, an autumn herring market) |
| Taxes and tithes were paid in kind | A levy of goods per season, beside the existing levy of days |
| Young people left farms to serve in towns | A population leak, or a way to send a pawn away for coin |

In real life a town trip took a farmer away for a few days. With 1 game day = 1 real week, a trip to Stockholm from the
outer archipelago is a fraction of a game day each way, so a market visit could fit into one game day.

## Kim's answers (27 November 2026)

1. **Period.** The game is set in Sweden, 1100–1500, with elements borrowed mainly from continental Europe. The main
   scenario starts with 1 or 2 villages and grows to many villages and 1 to 2 towns, about 1,000 pawns.
2. **Towns on the map.** Towns are seen on the map. There is a larger world off the map.
3. **Salt.** Yes: salt is a scarce import the village must trade for.
4. **Off-map trade.** Trade with the world off the map goes through a town.
5. **Which mechanics first.** Not answered; the mechanics are recorded as Proposed in `design.md`, section 17.

Follow-ups, the same day:

- The period borrows freely from other periods and from the continent.
- A single salt spring in the whole archipelago stays as a small local source.
- Towns exist before the ship: the ship visits an existing harbour of an existing town.
