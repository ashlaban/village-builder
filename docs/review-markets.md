# Review: how markets began, what the medieval market was, and what the fair should be

10 November 2026. Research for slice 4's plan (VB-183, the autumn fair), asked for by Kim before any design: how the first markets came
to be and what they did in early societies, how that compares with the market of medieval society, and what both mean for a game system
in which villages deal with each other and from which a town, and later a city, can grow. Nothing here is built or decided. `design.md`
is unchanged; section 5 lists what the research suggests changing, and section 6 the questions it raises.

It builds on the cities review (`docs/review-cities.md`), which covers what a town needs (its eight bases), towns in Scandinavia, the
town as a demographic sink and the elite. This review does not repeat those; it looks at the step before them, the market itself.

**How far to trust it.** Section 7 lists the sources. Claims marked (v) were checked against a source in this session, most of them
secondary (encyclopaedia articles, abstracts, one gazetteer's introduction). Claims marked (m) are from the standard literature as the
author remembers it, not re-read: treat their details (numbers, dates) as to be checked before they are quoted in `design.md`.

## 1. The short answer

1. **Inside a community, there was no market.** Neighbours and kin moved goods by gift, sharing and running debts; nobody has
   described a society that lived by barter (v). The game's kin help and goodwill are the historically right base, and the fair should
   not replace them inside a village.
2. **The market began at the edge.** Spot exchange, with haggling, was what people did with strangers: at the boundary between groups,
   on a beach, at a ford, and above all at gatherings held for other reasons (v, m). So the first "market" is a property of an
   assembly, not a facility.
3. **People came for the gathering; trade rode along.** Law, cult, games, matchmaking and news drew people from far; exchange used the
   crowd and the peace that the gathering imposed (v). The Disting at Uppsala was a thing, a sacrifice and a fair in one (v).
4. **A peace made it possible, and someone guaranteed it.** Strangers deal only where violence and cheating are held off: a sworn
   peace for the days of the gathering, later a lord's or king's market peace with a court (m). Whoever guarantees the peace gains
   from it. That is the seed of the patron, the cities review's base 7.
5. **Early trade was in differences, and in lumps.** What moved first between communities was what one had and the other lacked
   (salt, iron, stone, fish, furs), and things of rank (animals, ornaments, marriage partners). Food moved by other rules (v).
6. **The medieval market is a different thing: a regular, licensed, taxed institution that peasants needed.** It was weekly, within a
   walk, held by a lord for its tolls and court fines, and it existed because households owed rent, tax and tithe in coin and so had
   to sell (v). It was also where they bought what the household no longer made.
7. **The annual fair and the weekly market are two institutions, not two sizes of one.** The fair is seasonal, regional, for lumps
   and for strangers (livestock, cloth, hiring, credit settled from fair to fair). The market is weekly, local and for small things.
   A town grows where a market becomes frequent enough that someone can live beside it (m).
8. **Most markets failed.** Of English places with a market in 1300, about 39% were still market towns around 1600 (v). A game in
   which fairs compete and some die is truer, and gives the "about two cities a map" from geography without a quota.

For the game: build the fair as an **occasion first** (an assembly with a peace, where villages meet, court and settle things), with
exchange between strangers as what the occasion makes possible; let its **frequency**, not a flag, be what grows into a market and a
town; and expect the fair to stay small until something forces households to sell (obligations in slice 5, tithe and tax in slice 8).

## 2. How the first markets came to be

### 2.1 Before the market: gift, sharing and debt

- **No barter economy.** Caroline Humphrey (1985): "No example of a barter economy, pure and simple, has ever been described" (v).
  Graeber (*Debt*, 2011) builds on this: within a community people did not swap; they gave, and kept a loose account of who owed
  whom. Barter is what happens "between strangers, people who have no moral relations with one another" (v). Critics answer that
  absence of evidence is weak evidence (v), which leaves the positive finding standing: where we can see inside small communities,
  we see gift and credit.
- **Three ways to move goods.** Polanyi (*Trade and Market in the Early Empires*, 1957) names reciprocity (between equals bound by
  relation), redistribution (into a centre and out again: a chief's or temple's store) and market exchange, and argues that in most
  societies before the modern one the market was the least of the three (v). His claim in its strong form is disputed for
  Mesopotamia (section 2.4), but the three forms are a sound vocabulary. The game has reciprocity (kin help, goodwill) and a small
  redistribution (the village store).
- **Reciprocity changes with distance.** Sahlins (*Stone Age Economics*, 1972) (m): close kin give without counting (generalised);
  neighbours and distant kin expect a fair return in time (balanced); strangers haggle, cheat or raid (negative). The kind of exchange
  is a function of social distance. This is the game's allowance exactly: goodwill and opinion widen what one party will concede, and
  between strangers the allowance is near zero (target-v2, section 5.2).
- **The gift creates the tie.** Mauss (*The Gift*, 1925) (m): a gift obliges a return, and the standing debt is the relation; settling
  it exactly ends the relation. A market deal is one that leaves nothing owing, which is why it suits strangers and offends kin.

### 2.2 Exchange at the edge

- **Where the peace is thin, trade needs a device.** The oldest descriptions are of trade that avoids contact: goods laid on a beach
  and gold laid beside them (Herodotus on the Carthaginians) (m). Whatever its truth, it names the problem: exchange between groups
  with no shared law.
- **Partnerships.** In the Trobriand kula, ceremonial valuables pass between lifelong partners on other islands, never repaid at
  once; ordinary goods are bartered on the same voyages (*gimwali*), with haggling, and the two are kept apart: the kula "is not
  supposed to be conducted like gimwali" (v). The partner is a host and protector abroad, so the ceremonial tie is what makes the
  useful trade safe. Norse guest-friendship works the same way (m).
- **Spheres of exchange.** Among the Tiv (Bohannan 1955), goods fell into ranked spheres: food and household things; prestige goods
  (cattle, metal rods, cloth, slaves); and rights in people, above all marriage. Exchange within a sphere was morally neutral;
  converting upward (food into cattle) was admired, downward shameful, and one reading is that the spheres protected subsistence from
  those with wealth (v). General-purpose money collapsed the spheres (v). The stricter readings are disputed (v), but the pattern is
  common: **not everything may be turned into everything**, and what may is a matter of custom.
- **Markets need differences.** West African periodic markets ran on a shared "market week", each place on its day, traders moving
  round a ring (v, m). Where there was little trade between regions and little surplus, as in precolonial south-western West Africa,
  there were no periodic markets at all (v). Identical neighbours have little to sell each other.

### 2.3 Assemblies: the market as a side effect

- **The gathering comes first.** Early Irish *óenaig* were seasonal assemblies where "kings were proclaimed, laws were agreed, justice
  was administered, political alliances were concluded, marriages were made", with horse racing, games, feasting and markets; recent
  scholarship holds that reading them mainly as fairs gets them backwards (v). At Tailtiu, marriages were a noted feature (v).
- **The Disting.** The *Dísaþing* at Gamla Uppsala was the Thing of all Swedes, held with the sacrifice to the *dísir* at the end of
  February or early March; when the assembly was moved to Candlemas the name stayed with the great fair, held until it was abolished
  in 1895 and informally still (v). A winter date suits travel over snow and ice with loads (m: plausible and often said; not checked
  here). It is the game's winter fair (target-v2, trace 7.3), and it shows the order: **cult and law, then trade**.
- **England.** Before about 1200 "many markets were held on Sunday", having "developed out of these regular assemblies", often "in
  and around churchyards"; some fairs stood at "places of assembly or cult that were already ancient by 1100" (v). People were
  gathered already; the market was what they did afterwards.
- **Why this works.** An assembly solves the two problems of early exchange at once: it gathers enough people that a seller meets a
  buyer (a thin population cannot keep a market going), and it brings its own peace, since the thing or the feast is under oath or
  under a god.

### 2.4 Trading places, and the long-distance trade

- **Ports of trade.** Polanyi's term for a neutral place set apart from the country behind it, where foreigners traded under the
  ruler's protection and rules, for goods "which could not be obtained within the area" (v). Its point was partly to keep foreign
  trade from disturbing local life.
- **Kanesh.** The best-documented early case: Assyrian merchants from Assur, about 1950 to 1840 BCE, lived in a *kārum* (a quarter,
  literally a quay) below the Anatolian city of Kanesh, bringing tin and textiles by donkey and taking silver and gold home; more
  than 23,000 tablets survive, and the merchants "were independent, not representatives of their king" (v). They worked as family
  firms with treaties with local rulers, who took a toll and gave protection (m). Private profit-seeking trade is very old; it was
  **long-distance, in light and dear goods, by resident strangers under a local ruler's peace**.
- **Was Mesopotamia a market economy?** Polanyi said temple and palace redistributed and prices were set equivalences; Silver, Powell
  and others find prices that move, local markets, and sales of land and labour from the third millennium; a 2017 review calls the
  question unresolved because the records are opaque (v). The safe reading for a game: **redistribution, reciprocity and markets
  coexisted**, each for different goods and partners, and silver served as a unit of account long before most people handled it.
- **Emporia and nodal points.** In northern Europe, trading places appear in the 7th and 8th centuries. Hodges (*Dark Age Economics*,
  1982) sorted them into seasonal beach markets and planned, permanent ones under royal control (m). Sindbæk (2007) finds only a
  handful of true long-distance hubs in early Viking Age Scandinavia (Ribe, Kaupang, Birka, Åhus, Truso among them), placed where
  bulk traffic had to stop, and distinguishes them from central places such as Roskilde, tied to a local hinterland and an authority
  (v). Kaupang (the word means market place) was founded about 800 beside Skiringssal, a royal seat (v). So there are **two kinds of
  early market site**: the node on a long route, and the centre of a district; they need not be the same place, as the cities review
  found for the harbour and the central place (its section 3.4).

### 2.5 What the first markets did

| Role | How |
| --- | --- |
| Moved what could not be had at home | Salt, metal, stone, fish, furs: differences of land, not surpluses of the same thing |
| Made strangers into partners | Trade partners, guest-friends, in-laws; the standing tie is the safe-conduct |
| Found spouses | An assembly is the marriage market of everyone within reach (Skinner's marketing community, section 3.2) |
| Carried news and settled disputes | The thing or fair is where cases, debts and agreements across communities are made public |
| Displayed and converted rank | Feasting, gifts and prestige goods; turning food into animals and animals into alliances |
| Paid someone | The host or protector takes gifts, a toll or first choice of goods |

Feeding people who did not farm was not among the first roles. That came when something forced or drew staple food to a centre:
tribute, rent and tax first, sale second.

## 3. The market in medieval society

### 3.1 A franchise

- **Numbers.** In England, 2,466 markets and 2,768 fairs are recorded by 1516 at 2,254 places; about 57% of the markets date from the
  13th century; "the number of markets and fairs granted rose sharply in the thirteenth century, declined after the mid fourteenth
  century" (v). Many older ones were "held by custom", without a grant (v).
- **A right, held by a lord.** A grant gave a named person or house, usually in heredity, "the right to hold and to control these
  events and to collect from them revenues, including tolls and the profits of jurisdiction", only if it did no harm to neighbouring
  markets (v). Lords founded markets as investments; the income "may not have been significant" for many (v), and the hope of a town
  on the lord's land was part of the point (m).
- **Spacing.** "Six and two-thirds miles" was the 13th-century rule of thumb for "neighbouring" (v): a day's journey of twenty miles
  in three parts, to walk there, to trade and to walk home (m, Bracton's reasoning). The medieval market's catchment is the **half-day
  round trip**, not the day's walk of the fair.
- **Failure.** About 72% of places with a market in 1200 were market towns around 1600, but only 39% of those with one in 1300;
  closely spaced markets were most at risk (v). The landscape was over-supplied and then thinned out.
- **Law on the spot.** Disputes at a market or fair went to its own court, sitting "before the mayor and bailiffs of the borough, or
  the steward", for contracts and offences arising at that fair (v): quick justice for people who would be gone tomorrow. With it
  came a peace over the market and the roads to it, standard measures, and rules of the moral economy: the assizes of bread and ale,
  and bans on buying up goods before they reached the stalls (forestalling) or to resell (regrating) (m).

### 3.2 Two institutions and a hierarchy

- **The weekly market** served a few miles: grain, ale, bread, eggs, cheese, small livestock, pots, shoes, the smith. Peasants sold
  in small lots and bought the same day. Much of its custom was women's (m).
- **The annual fair** served a region for a few days: livestock driven in on the hoof, wool and cloth, iron, salt, horses, the hiring
  of servants for the year, and debts made at one fair due at the next (m). After the Black Death regional fairs multiplied while
  village markets thinned (Epstein 1994, by its argument as cited; the article was not read here).
- **Periodicity is the hinge.** A trader needs a minimum of custom to live; a customer will walk only so far. Where the people within
  walking range are too few to keep a trader, the trader moves and the market is periodic; as demand thickens, market days multiply
  until the trader can stay (Stine 1962; Skinner 1964) (m). **A town is a market that never closes.** This gives a ladder with a
  cause: a yearly fair, a seasonal fair, a weekly market, a daily market with resident craftsmen.
- **The market area is a society.** Skinner (1964) argued that the Chinese peasant's social world was not the village but the
  standard marketing area, some eighteen villages round one market town, sharing dialect, cult and kin (v, m). His stronger claim,
  that marriages were made within it through market acquaintance, has been questioned: a county study found the marriage area did
  fall inside the marketing area, but matches were arranged, not made at market (v). Either way, **the market's reach bounds the
  pool of in-laws**, which is what the game's marriage market lacks (cities review, section 3.5; VB-126).

### 3.3 Why peasants went

- **To raise what they owed.** Peasants sold "grain, eggs, cheese, animals, or ale to obtain cash for dues, tools, salt, cloth,
  taxes, fees, or marriage expenses"; the poor "had to sell as soon as the crops were harvested, when the price was lowest" (v).
  Rent, tax and tithe in coin are what turned a household that could feed itself into one that had to trade.
- **Not for most of their food.** Even in south-eastern England peasants sold less than half their grain (v). The household stayed
  the unit of subsistence; the market took the margin and supplied what it lacked.
- **Credit ran through it.** Most village dealing was on trust, reckoned in money and settled later or in kind (m): the market did
  not replace the running account between neighbours, it priced it.

### 3.4 Sweden and the North

- **Trade belonged to towns, by law.** Magnus Eriksson's town law (about 1350) banned *landsköp*, dealing in merchants' goods (salt,
  cloth, spices) in the countryside, so that "the burghers' monopoly on trade arose"; the yearly fairs were excepted (v). The ban had
  to be repeated for centuries, so it was broken: Köping was once called Laglösaköping (v).
- **And yet peasants traded.** The land law excepted Norrland, Finland, Gotland and Öland, and trade for one's own necessities;
  peddling one's own handicraft was allowed (v). Peasants sailed their own goods to town (*bondeseglation*) (m). Gotland's farmers
  were themselves sea traders (*farmenn*) with a house at Novgorod, until Visby taxed their goods and shut them out of foreign trade:
  the war of 1288 was between the town and its own countryside (v).
- **Privileged middlemen.** The *birkarlar* held from the 13th century a recognised monopoly of trade and tax among the Sami (v):
  trade across a cultural boundary, by licensed partners, as in section 2.2.

## 4. The two compared

| | The first markets | The medieval market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A side of a gathering (thing, feast, cult), or a neutral place on a route | A franchise: a right to hold a market at a place on a day |
| Who meets | Strangers and partners from different communities | Neighbours of a district; at fairs, the region and outsiders |
| How often | Yearly or seasonal; when the ship or caravan comes | Weekly (market), yearly (fair), daily in towns |
| What moves | What the other side lacks; things of rank; spouses, news | Staples in small lots, craft goods, livestock, labour |
| How it is valued | Custom, partnership and haggling; ranked spheres | Money as unit of account; prices that move, inside rules of fairness |
| What makes it safe | The gathering's sworn peace; a host; a partner | Market peace, a court on the spot, measures, a lord's or king's grant |
| Who gains | The host or chief: gifts, prestige, first choice | The holder: tolls, stall rents, fines; the town's burghers by monopoly |
| Why people come | For the gathering; for what cannot be had at home | Because they owe coin, and to buy what the household no longer makes |
| Relation to food | Marginal; staples move by sharing and tribute | Central to the town's supply; the margin of the peasant's harvest |
| Place in society | At the edge of the community, kept apart from its inner life | Inside it, weekly, but fenced by custom and law |

**What carried over.** The peace; the patron who guarantees it and gains; the court; the tie of market to assembly and church; the
yearly fair for lumps and strangers; credit and reputation beneath every deal; a line between what may be sold and what may not.

**What changed.** Frequency, and so permanence; who is obliged to come; money as the common measure, which dissolved the spheres; the
market's site becoming property; and the town's claim to be the only place where trade is lawful.

**What the change needed.** A denser countryside; lords and a church taking part of the harvest and wanting it in coin; coin itself;
and goods the household stopped making. These are the cities review's bases 3, 4, 6 and 7, in the order the roadmap builds them.

## 5. What it means for the game

Each point says what the history suggests, how it sits with the design as it stands, and where it would change it. They are
proposals for discussion, not text for `design.md`.

1. **The fair is an occasion before it is an exchange.** Roadmap 3.4 lists "encounters at the venue, so villages mix" last, as a
   benefit. History puts it first: the draw is the gathering, and with identical self-sufficient farms the trade alone may not be
   worth a day's walk. Proposal: the fair's draw has social terms beside need (courting for the unmarried, kin in other villages,
   a case to bring), and its first measure of success is marriages and ties across villages, with rations traded second. This
   raises VB-126 (occasions) from something the fair uses to something the fair is built on.
2. **Inside a village, goodwill; between villages, the swap.** Section 2.1 supports the design's two valuations and allowance as
   they stand, and adds a prediction: deals between strangers are spot deals with small residuals, so the fair's first trades are
   those where both gain by need, and many fail on lumps (the probe "deals failed for want of change" is well chosen). What history
   adds is the **trade partner**: repeated fair dealing builds goodwill between two holders of different villages, and that tie is
   what later carries a loan, a marriage or a guest's lodging. Goodwill's 32 counterparts and its decay already allow this; the fair
   should prefer last year's partner when proposing.
3. **What is there to trade? Insurance now, differences later.** The cities review measured surplus and shortfall in the same
   years, which is a case for the fair as risk pooling across households and villages. That is real, but it is the thinnest reason
   markets existed (section 2.2: no differences, no markets). The durable reasons are goods of place. The map already has shores,
   forests and plains; if a shore village had fish or salt, a forest village tar, timber or bog iron, and a plain village grain,
   villages would need each other before any ship came. Roadmap puts salt and iron with the ship (slice 8). Question 2 below asks
   whether one local difference should come with the fair.
4. **Spheres, not one worth table.** The plan has one table in rations (a cow 40, a sheep 6, a day's work 3). History suggests
   ranked spheres: food; animals, cloth and labour; land and rights in people (marriage, service), with conversion between them
   governed by custom and morally charged. The design already leans this way ("Debts are paid with": labour; land). Proposal: keep
   the one unit of account, and make **which conversions are proper** a norm, so that selling grain for a cow in a good year is
   admired, taking a cow for grain in a dearth is booked as a hard bargain (target-v2, 5.4), and land is not on the table at a fair
   at all until slice 5's custom allows it.
5. **A peace, and someone who keeps it.** Every market in both periods rests on a guaranteed peace and a way to settle disputes on
   the spot. Proposal: the fair has a **host** (the village on whose land it stands, through its alderman or leading holder) who
   keeps the fair's peace and hears its cases, and gains by it: prestige first, and later a share (a toll, a gift, stall rent). This
   gives the fair a patron from its first year, puts the alderman's road (target-v2, 7.1) through the fair, and makes "who holds the
   fair" something villages can want and contest. It also answers "who books" for the village (The gift, Who books).
6. **The site: a district's centre, on ground that is nobody's or the host's.** Highest market potential (cities review, 3.4) picks
   a central place, which is right for the district fair. History adds two qualifications. Early sites were often on boundaries or
   at an older gathering place, and the node on the long route (the harbour) is often a different place, to be found when the ship
   comes. And sites were not fixed by decree: several were tried and most failed. Proposal: a fair is called by a village that
   stands to gain; more than one may arise on a map; a fair that draws too few lapses. Potential then explains which survive
   instead of choosing one.
7. **Frequency is the tier.** The cities review defines a market place by a count of trading households. History suggests the
   count is the result and frequency the cause: a fair that fills gains a second date (spring, before sowing; midwinter where ice
   opens the way), then a market day each month or week, and a household can live beside it once the days are close enough to feed
   it. Proposal: slice 4 builds one yearly fair, but stores its calendar as a list of dates, and slice 9's tier reads that list
   (with hysteresis, as target-v2 4.6 asks), so that the town grows out of the fair by one quantity.
8. **The fair will stay small until households must sell.** Peasants marketed the margin, and what made the margin large was
   obligation. In the game no household owes anything outside its kin, so the fair's volume will be bounded by lumps, floors and
   small allowances. That is a correct result and should not be tuned away: slice 5's debts, and slice 8's tithe and tax, are what
   make the market weekly. It supports the roadmap's order (fair, debt, mill, ship, town), and it means slice 4's success is not
   "much grain moved".
9. **Fair and market are different things; build the fair.** Slice 4's goods (cattle, sheep, wool, labour days, grain by the load)
   and its loans due "at the next harvest" are the fair's, correctly. The hiring of servants for the year belongs here too: it fits
   service (VB-090) and the moving round, which already falls a few days after. The weekly market in small things is slice 9's.
10. **Customs to expect.** Each rule of section 3.1 is a custom with positions that can drift and split by village, which is the
    game's strength: who may trade at the fair (anyone; holders; men); what may be sold (the spheres of point 4); whether dealing
    outside the fair is proper (the *landsköp* question); what a fair price is in a dearth; how debts are paid. Slice 4 needs at
    most one of them built; the rest are named so that `Culture.norm` has room.
11. **Town against country.** Gotland in 1288 is a ready-made late-game conflict: once a town holds the right to trade, the
    farmers who used to sail their own goods are in its way. Nothing to build now; a note for slices 8 and 9.
12. **Cost.** Nothing above adds a loop over pairs. Encounters are among those present at one venue for three days, a declared cap
    per pawn (rule 2); proposals in seeded order to a few partners, last year's first; the fair's dates are a due-queue (rule 4);
    several candidate fairs are a handful of yearly searches, one per calling village.

## 6. Questions this raises

To be put to Kim before `design.md` is revised; the plan's own questions (goods, quanta, need curves, loan terms, boats) follow
after.

1. Is the fair first of all an assembly (people come to meet; trade follows), or first of all an outlet for surplus (roadmap 3.4)?
2. Should one good of place (fish or salt from shore villages, say) arrive with the fair, so that villages differ, or do all
   differences wait for the ship?
3. Does the fair have a host who keeps its peace and gains from it, from the first year?
4. One fair at the site of highest potential, fixed for good, or fairs that are called, compete and may lapse?
5. One worth table, or spheres with conversions as a custom?
