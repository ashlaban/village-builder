Village builder · balance map · reflection after eight experiments · 12 November 2026

What the mocks say about the balance map

Eight experiments tested draft 3 of the map: five mock models, measurements on the real game, a survey of abstractions, and accounts built at the target. The loop diagram turned out to be the wrong main picture, and several things it shows are not what holds this game in balance.

In short

  • The map could not be simulated as drawn. Only 3 of its 40 links could be turned into a model from the sign alone. What was missing was the same each time: quantities that add up, gates with thresholds, the spread between households, and time.
  • Marriage holds the population, not hunger. Hunger touches under 1% of households in the game. The dial that matters is how much a home must have before a couple may marry.
  • The surplus already exists, and about a fifth of the harvest rots. The target needs 1.7 to 2.15 times the farmers' need, depending on rot and feasting, against 1.64 to 1.86 measured.
  • The road from hunger to a landed elite carries almost no traffic. In the mocks an elite of the right size comes from a tithe and offices. Debt concentrates land only after the map is full, and slowly.
  • Growth is slower than the vision. The ceiling is about 2.4% a year; reaching 1,000 in 150 years needs about 3.1%.

Every mock uses the game's own numbers where they exist and invented ones elsewhere. Treat directions and orders of size as findings, and exact percentages as illustrations.

What changed in the picture of the game

1. The marriage gate is the master dial

The game already delays marriage for want of a home: couples putting it off rose from none to 50 to 120 a decade, and almost nobody stays unmarried. In the whole-map model, switching this gate off left 72% more people and 64% of households hungry. Its setpoint, the means a home must have, sets the food ratio late in the game, and through it land concentration, the town's food and hunger. Draft 3 has no place for that number.

  • Every way of holding 1,000 has a human cost. By hunger alone about half of children die. By marriage at 18 alone about half never marry. Marriage at 26 cost the fewest lives.
  • Balance held by marriage overshoots after a shock and echoes for a generation. Balance held by hunger or disease returns smoothly.
  • The town does not slow growth. Its migrants are the village's unmarried, so the brake is still the marriage gate. The town decides where people live.

2. The food is there; the question is where it goes

  • 1.67 is a floor. It assumes no rot, no feasting, an elite that eats like peasants and a town that grows nothing. With today's rot the need is about 1.96, or 2.15 if half the surplus is feasted.
  • Rot is the decisive dial. It has to fall from about 17% of output to about 3%, and farmers have to sell nearly nine tenths of what they have spare.
  • The town is the residual claimant. It eats what is left after everyone else, so a 2% fall in yield already leaves it short.
  • About 15% of today's produce has no outlet in the measured split. Only forcing the accounts to close found it. It should be checked in the game.

3. The elite comes from tithe and office, not from debt

  • Rent cannot feed an elite. A tithe falls on everything every field produces; rent falls on the grain of tenant fields only. Rent and interest come to 6 to 10% of output at most. Living on rent alone would need nine tenths of all fields under tenancy.
  • Debt concentrates land only when the map is full and food is tight. That is why two round 1 models disagreed: one assumed a full island from year 1, the other never ran short. At the game's food ratio debt gives no more than one elite household in 150 years.
  • The road from hunger through goodwill to land moved 70 fields in 250 years. Hard lenders, not generous households, end up with land. Patronage as proposed is negligible, because the owed work is cleared in a year or two.
  • An elite by office has no brake. It moves one for one with the tithe. That makes it easy to tune and gives it no dynamics.
  • The design's rule for "landed" miscounts. It counted 45 to 100 of 200 households as landed with no rents at all.

4. Growth needs help from outside

  • With the game's numbers the ceiling is 2.4% a year. Founders alone grow about 1 to 1.7% and reach about 100 people by year 150, mostly because kinship bars marriages.
  • Faster demography alone (earlier marriage, shorter nursing, fewer child deaths) reaches about 565 and overshoots into hunger.
  • One immigrant family every two years reaches about 1,000 by year 150. That is more than "very limited".

5. Reach is the condition under every limit

You said reach by water is not a lever. The measurements agree and go further. Growth slows when land within a day's walk of a village runs out, with only 14% of the map in use. Walking and carrying take 30 to 47% of working hours outside winter. Reach decides where the land limit sits, how large a town's catchment is, and who can marry whom.

6. Culture needs a seed and a switch

  • An opinion penalty keeps differences but does not create them. Two cultures need a seed: different starting customs, or different land.
  • It acts as a switch. Marriages across the divide fell from 26% to 1% between two nearby settings.
  • Your rise and fall needed three things: poorer land on one island, land running out pushing the heirs custom, and attitudes pulled toward the practice in force. The game has no such pull, and its customs flicker: 29 to 46% of changes undo the previous one.
  • The fall came from the town and a Church doctrine, which opinion does not filter.

The two round 2 experiments disagree on the single picture. One wants the food ladder at the centre; the other wants the regime strip. I think they answer different questions. The budget says whether the target is possible and who goes short. The regime says which dial matters at this point in a game. A designer tuning needs the second to know where to look and the first to know what a change costs.

The vocabulary the map needs

Draft 3 has two kinds of thing, properties and levers. The experiments needed seven:

KindWhat it isExamples
StockSomething that accumulates, with a target bandFarmers, townspeople, elite; grain in store; goodwill; land held and tenanted
FlowWhat moves between stocks or to a sink; flows add upBirths, deaths, moves to town; eaten, taken, sold, feasted, rotted
GateA threshold that opens or closes a flowA home with means; hunger at 0.5 stops marriage; the floor; default after two harvests
SetpointThe number that says where a gate or rate sits. This is what gets tunedThe means test, the tithe, rot rates, yield, how much farmers want goods
ConditionSets where limits sit; not tuned during a gameReach on foot and by water; ships; the outside world
Process between householdsMoves things within a class; invisible in class totalsKin help, feasts, loans, patronage
ConsequenceFollows from the rest; watched, not targetedIdle hours; the food ratio needed; land inequality

Draft 3 mixed these. Births and deaths were drawn as levers but are flows. Reach by water was a lever but is a condition. Food produced was a property but is a rate with a setpoint under it. Idle hours was a property to tune but is the slack a household keeps once its need is met.

This keeps what draft 3 did well: the five-way split of the harvest stays the centre. It adds what the experiments needed: stocks with targets, gates as their own symbol, conditions outside the frame, and a time axis. Culture would sit as dials on four of the gates (heirs, marriage rules, who eats first, who speaks), with its own small curve on the era strip.

Decisions the experiments put to you

  1. Where should the elite come from? Tithe and office gives the 75 easily and without drama. Debt gives a slow, late ratchet won by hard lenders. Your road from hunger through goodwill gives almost nothing under the designed numbers. If that road matters to the vision, it needs a mechanism that does not depend on households going short.
  2. How should the island reach 1,000? More immigration than "very limited", a larger start, a longer game, or easier marriage for founders. Faster births alone overshoot.
  3. What holds farmers at 600 when land does not bind? The target uses about 5% of a medium map. Either reach does it, or the elite withholds land, or the land fills with pasture and woods.
  4. Which cost is acceptable? Late marriage, a deadly town, child deaths and emigration are interchangeable ways to stop growth. The mix is a design choice with a human face.

To verify in the game

  • The 15% of produce with no outlet in the measured split.
  • The village store: 68 to 85% of the grain put in rots, and little is given back out.
  • The "landed" rule counting households with no rents.
  • Founders stalling at about 1% a year, with a third of households single-person.
  • Customs flickering back and forth; no pull toward the practice in force.
  • The game cannot yet report: share extracted, tenancy, town share, elite share, trade, goodwill from feasts, disease.

The experiments: a demography cohort model; a 200-household model of surplus, debt and estates; draft 3 as one stock-and-flow model; a culture model of 12 villages; measurements on the populous and founders scenarios; a survey of abstractions; accounts at the target; and a household model of the whole 200-year arc with a blind test of the dial table.
