# VB-702: the metals and the stones, what each deposit is for (16 November 2026)

The plan, after the session with Kim on 16 November 2026. Phase one was a draft with eight choices; Kim answered them, and one answer
became principle 17 (`AGENTS.md`, Deciding without Kim): a good is what it is for and is made of a material; the kind makes a better
or worse one, not a different good. The plan is reworked around it: the head table is goods by purpose (section 2), rocks and metals
are kinds with properties (section 3), and section 4 says how a good carries its kind in state. The design is written into
`design.md` (Goods, production and animals: Goods, recipes and facilities; The mill and the loom; In the ground; What each good is
for), the tasks are VB-733 to VB-745 (section 10), and two choices are left for Kim (section 11). The measure is
`docs/plans/VB-702-reach.js`, a throwaway script, not game code. History below is from general reading, not checked by search, and
marked where it is unsure; every recipe number is Proposed.

kind (section 4). Numbers are Proposed starting values. "Built" and the task IDs say where each stands.

What the recast changed against the draft:
- The soapstone vessel was "a kind of VB-366's pot" by principle 4; it is now a kind of the pot by principle 17, and the cast bronze
  pot is the third kind of the same good, not a good of its own. One good, three kinds, three lives: the head's choice is clay that
  must be refired every few years, soapstone from another village that lasts a generation, or bronze that outlives the house.
- The kettle stays a good of its own: its purpose differs (it is what a brewing is boiled in, read by what it holds), and copper is
  its one kind.
- Marble no longer waits for the church as a whole. It is a kind of stone: it builds as any stone, burns to lime as limestone does,
  and is the finest kind of the memorial stone. The church's own uses (fonts, altars) wait for slice 8.
- Amber is not stone: it is a material of its own with one kind, and its good is the thing of worth, which it shares with silver
  and gold. It enters with them (principle 4: a new kind of a good is a row); rosaries and incense wait for the church.
- The whetstone and the grindstone are two goods (a hone in the hand, a wheel that is turned), as Kim confirmed: slate and sandstone
  are kinds of the first, sandstone the only kind of the second.
- Granite's role is unchanged: the kind nearly everyone has, and the poorer kind of the quern.

Whetstones, for information (the orchestrator's memory, unchecked): the prized Norse whetstones were fine-grained quartz-mica schist
(Eidsborg, light grey; and a dark purple-grey phyllite), traded across northern Europe from the Viking Age; sandstone is coarser,
cutting fast but leaving a rougher edge, and suited to rotary grindstones; true roofing slate is a middling hone. The game's "slate"
stands for the fissile fine-grained metamorphic rocks.

## 4. How a good carries its kind

**What is already Decided, and what principle 17 adds.** "Goods are typed with subtypes (granite and marble are both stone)" is
Decided (3 Nov; `design.md`, Goods, recipes and facilities), and a recipe that "may bind the subtype it was given" is Proposed
(target-v2, 4.3). Grain by crop is built so (VB-347). Principle 17 adds two things: the subtype travels from the material into the
good made of it (a whetstone of slate, not only a load of slate), and the subtype is where quality lives. Target-v2's
`Lot.quality?: number` was the hook for "the quality of goods" (its gap, 10.3; `design.md`, Open questions): Kim's confirmation (no
single grade number) answers it, and the hook is not used.

**Words.** Principle 17 says class and kind; the registry (`sim/stores`, VB-299) already has `kind` (the good), `subtype`, and
`class` (custom's class of exchange: food, wares). So in code the good is the registry's `kind` ('whetstone'), principle 17's kind
is its `subtype` ('slate'), the id is `whetstone/slate`, and the class of material is a new field, `material` ('stone'). `class`
keeps its meaning.

**The registry** (owner: `sim/stores`, the goods registry; VB-416 splits it out as a module of its own). A good declares its
`material` and its kinds, a small fixed list (at most 8), each with the properties the good reads, as named tunables of
`sim/rules.js` (principle 3: each has a physical meaning). Declared at load and sealed with the rules registry; code, never saved
state. The properties are compiled at load into one frozen row a good and kind, so a reader in a loop does a lookup and allocates
nothing (Rules for cost, 6). A query is one good and kind (`pot/soapstone`) or any kind of a good (`pot`), as `grain/barley` and
`grain` are today.

**State.** Plain data, in three shapes, all of them already in the code:
1. *Counted, by kind:* a store's count of a good is `{[kind]: n}`, as `household.grain` is `{[crop]: rations}`. The stone heap
   (`household.stone`, today one number that "keeps no kinds"), ore, metal, pots and things of worth are held so. A put names its
   kind (the tile's bedrock, for quarried stone); a take of one kind takes it; a take of any kind falls on the kind of least
   customary worth first (a head does not lay marble in a foundation).
2. *One durable a household, read in closed form:* `{kind, made}`, as `household.quern` is `{rock, made}`: the whetstone, the
   grindstone, the kettle. It is worn out at `made` plus the kind's life; no pass wears it (Rules for cost, 3).
3. *A living thing* stays what it is (cattle by head).

There are no per-item records: a household holds counts, and at most one record a durable good. Worth is one row of custom's table
a good and kind (`Culture.norm('worth', 'pot/soapstone')`); the kind's properties never compute it (principle 17: worth follows from
custom).

**Recipes.** A recipe binds the kind of its material into its product (a load of `stone/slate` hewn into `whetstone/slate`); where
only some kinds will do, the recipe's query lists them (the grindstone takes sandstone). An alloy is a recipe with two metals in
and a third kind out (copper and tin to bronze).

**Cost, named (rule 9).** A household gains about 30 numbers: stone by 6 kinds, ore by 5, metal by 7, pots by 3, things of worth by
3, and 3 durables of two fields each. At `medium-ref` (about 250 households) that is under 10,000 numbers in a save that pawns and
tiles dominate: no change to save or load that could be measured. A reader of "any stone" sums 6 numbers, as `grainOf` sums the
crops. Nothing is added to a step: a property is read where a job or a planning day already runs. The fair's worth table gains
about 20 rows. No pass over pairs, no daily pass, no list of items.

**What it touches of the Decided.** Nothing is replaced: subtypes are Decided, the quern's two kinds are Decided, "quarried stone
is of the tile's bedrock" is Decided. Two points are left for Kim (section 11): whether a maker's skill may also make a better good
(skills are Decided, 11 Nov, as rate only), and whether granite may be a slower millstone (sandstone is Decided, 11 Nov).

**What slice 6's plan and its tasks assume.** VB-360 (skills) gives rate only: unchanged. VB-361 (facilities) is not touched: a
facility has a kind and a condition, and its stages are recipes, which may bind a kind (a roof of slate or of tiles). VB-366 keeps
`hh.pots` as a number: it is built as counts by kind from the start if VB-733 has landed, else VB-744 turns it into them when the
second kind arrives. VB-701 declares `iron`: it becomes `metal/iron` with VB-738. VB-698 (a target on every stored good) reads the
registry's rows: a target is set on a good, or on one kind of it where the head wants that kind.

## 7. The metals

### 7.1 Bronze, and copper on its own (Kim's answers 1 to 3)

History (medieval Nordic, 1100 to 1500). Iron made the tools; copper alloys made what iron could not: cooking cauldrons and kettles
(the three-legged cast *malmgryta* is one of the commonest finds of medieval Swedish households), brewing and dairy kettles of sheet
copper, buckles, brooches, scale weights, candlesticks, and the church's bells, cast in a pit beside the church by travelling bell
founders. Copper came from a few great mines (Falun, worked from about the year 1000 and big from the 13th century; I am unsure of
the earliest date), tin by sea from England and later Saxony. So bronze never came first: the Bronze Age was two thousand years
gone, and a medieval village's iron was its own while its bronze came from far off.

What it is for in the game, beside iron:
- **Copper alone, the kettle.** A sheet-copper kettle is the household's brewing vessel, later the dairy's and the dyer's. A brewing
  without one gives a barrel (slice 4: 10 rations of grain and 4 hours); with one it gives two for 6 hours, so the kettle is read by
  what it holds. It wears through in about 30 years, and a new one is beaten from 2 bars of copper (16 hours, skill smithing): the
  recurring sink. Copper alone needs no second place, so a copper village has a good of its own (D2).
- **Bronze, the cast pot.** The third kind of the pot (section 2): cast from 2 bars of bronze in a mould of a load of clay (12
  hours, skill casting). It does not break, so it is worth by custom what twenty clay pots are: a dowry good and an heirloom,
  inherited as movable worth (VB-276; the heirs' shares). It also covers the store as any pot does.
- **The bell.** The village's: its meeting decides to have one, the alderman carries it out (principle 16; VB-673): he asks the
  holders for the metal, a caster casts it in a pit by the green (20 bars of bronze, 60 hours), and it gives the village's holders a
  term of standing at the gathering. A bell cracks rarely (a hundredth a year) and is recast from its own metal with a tenth lost.
  Its use by the church waits for slice 8.
- **Iron first.** Iron (VB-701, with the fair, from the bog) is a need: tools that wear. Bronze is plenty and standing (principle 1:
  a pot or a bell is how plenty shows). Bronze is no kind of tool.
- **Two places, one good.** Bronze is an alloy: a recipe with copper and tin in (`BRONZE.tin`, a seventh by weight, Proposed) and
  bronze out, made by whoever holds both. Copper and tin each move by the load to the caster (nothing teleports).
- **Tin, twice as likely** (Kim's answer 3): section 6's trial, and VB-734.
- **Pewter** (tin and lead) was a late medieval town craft; it waits for the town (D9).

### 7.2 Silver, gold and amber: things of worth (Kim's answers 4 and 7)

History. Before and well after the first Nordic coins (about 1000; steady coinage in Sweden from the 13th century), silver went by
weight: hack silver weighed on folding scales, the provincial laws' fines and wergild counted in marks and öre of silver, dowries and
morning gifts given in silver, ornaments worn as a household's worth, and hoards buried and often never recovered (Gotland's hundreds
of hoards). Silver came with lead, from the same ore (Sala, from the 15th century; earlier mostly from abroad). Gold was marginal in
the north: finger rings, the church's plate.

One good, the thing of worth, of three kinds: silver and gold held by weight (an *öre*; worn as rings and brooches, cut as hack
silver), and amber as a string of beads. Worth by custom, Proposed: an öre of silver 20 rations (half a cow), gold 200, a string of
amber 10. What a household does with them before coin:
- **Keeps.** They neither spoil nor wear: a household turns a surplus it cannot eat into worth that waits. This is also their danger
  to principle 1 (the feast should be where plenty goes), so a thing of worth is got only from someone who has it, by a swap at its
  customary worth like any good, and the head's target for it (VB-698) is "what I mean to give", not "all I can". Barter until coin
  stands (10 Nov): silver is not change.
- **Gives.** The dowry and the morning gift (VB-276) carried in silver, which goes with the bride; a gift to bind a follower
  (VB-708); a fine paid (slice 5's cases). Each is a crossing between households, and each is carried.
- **Wears.** Shown on the pawn as an icon (principle 15); a term of standing with a cap, as the well-kept house's is: 0.02 for each
  20 rations' worth worn, at most 0.15 (Proposed).
- **Buries, and loses: the sink.** A hoard is buried by a pawn, an act, at its house; where it lies is the pawn's knowledge, told to
  the heir at death if the pawn could tell, else lost with it. The metal stays on the tile (nothing vanishes) and may be found
  again by work there, as a vein is. Grounded in Gotland's hoards; a story for the record (D5, and its principle candidate).
- **Gold** is the same rows at ten times the worth; it is dug as metal, not smelted. **Amber** is gathered on a beach that holds
  it, as clay is dug, and bored into beads at home (4 hours a string); a string breaks and scatters now and then (a fiftieth a
  year), its small sink.
- **The ship's silver (VB-188).** The ship brings silver and, with an established Crown, coin: the island's own silver is then a
  stock already in households, which the Crown mints first. Slice 8's plan reads it so.
- **Lead** comes out of the same ore as silver and is stored (nothing vanishes); it has no use until the church's roofs and
  windows and the town's pewter (D9).

### 7.3 The veins: how one is mined (Kim's answer 5)

History. Medieval Swedish mining (Bergslagen, Falun) was done by peasant miners (*bergsmän*) who farmed as well, in open pits and
shallow shafts, breaking the rock by fire-setting: a fire built against the face, the rock cracked as it cooled, then hammer and wedge;
it ate wood by the cartload. The ore was roasted and smelted near the mine in a small furnace (the *hytta*; Lapphyttan, about 1200, is
the oldest known blast furnace), owned in shares by the farms that worked it. So: hands first, a pit, wood; the furnace is the
facility.

- **A pawn digs, as clay is dug.** A found vein (`sim/prospect` knows) on the household's land, its village's outland or the
  wilderness (`sim/dig`'s `mayDig`) is dug a load a trip. No facility for an open pit: the tile's loads left are the pit, and it
  gets harder as it goes down. The job takes firewood from the household's pile for the fire-setting: 3 hours and 1 load of wood
  for a load of rock ore at the surface, 6 hours and 1.5 loads below half of what the tile began with (Proposed).
- **Who owns the ore:** whoever dug it, as with clay.
- **Ore is heavy, so smelting is near the mine.** A load of ore carried home 12 steps is a trip; a farm 30 steps off carries
  metal, not ore. This is principle 9's mechanism: the village near the vein smelts and trades metal (D4).
- **The furnace is a facility** (VB-361), built by a household beside its farm. Rock iron ore goes to the bloomery (VB-364) at a
  better yield than bog ore: ore is a good whose kinds differ in richness (section 2). Copper, tin and lead-silver ore need the
  furnace (VB-738).
- **A mine in shares** (the *bergslag*: those who work it hold shares, and the furnace is theirs together) comes later, as a kind
  of body after slice 5's bodies are built: a line of the design, not a task yet.
- **Prospecting as work** (In the ground: "waits for the first good that needs ore") comes now: a head that wants ore, and knows
  no vein, sends a pawn to the uplands for a day (a verb; the chance is `sim/prospect`'s).
- **Amounts.** In the ground says a village's vein should last about three generations once a year's use is named. At a mining
  household's 10 to 20 loads a year, a vein of 2 to 5 tiles at today's amounts (copper 150 to 800 a tile) lasts 20 to 200 years:
  about right, for VB-736 to check.

## 11. Choices left for Kim

1. **Does a maker's skill make a better good, or only make it faster?** Skills are Decided (11 Nov) as rate: a novice works at 60%,
   a master at 130%. Principle 17 puts quality in the kind. (a) Rate only: two whetstones of slate are alike, whoever cut them. (b)
   Skill also lengthens the life of a durable (the one record a household: whetstone, grindstone, quern, kettle), by up to a fifth
   for a master; counted goods stay alike. (c) Every lot carries a grade. **Proposed: (a) now**, and (b) when "the smith" should
   matter to buyers: it costs one number on a record that exists. (c) is the single grade number Kim set aside.
2. **May a millstone be granite, grinding slower?** Decided (11 Nov): a mill needs a pair of sandstone millstones, "so a village may
   have to trade before it can build". By principle 17 the quern's rule would carry over: granite, a fifth slower. (a) Sandstone
   only, as Decided: place decides who mills (principle 9), and a stone that sheds grit at a mill's speed is a physical limit. (b)
   Granite too, a fifth slower: most villages could build a mill unaided, and sandstone sells the better one. **Proposed: (a).**

## 12. What this plan does not settle

- The numbers of every recipe (hours, loads, wear, worth) are Proposed from general reading, not checked by search; each task finds

- The scarcity is of a populous start; a run's villages that settle later, nearer the hills, are not counted. VB-735 shows it at
  any year.
- Whether the western shores of a Nordic setting had amber at all (it is Baltic); the generator places it on any sandy shore.
- Falun's earliest working, how long a soapstone vessel lasted in use, and the whetstone note of section 2: unsure or unchecked.
- The worth of an öre of silver against a cow is a guess; the provincial laws would give it, and slice 8's plan (coin) must check it.
