## 2. What exists

**The generator** (`src/gen/geology.js`, VB-357). Bedrock on every land tile, by province: granite (weight 45), limestone (22),
sandstone (18), slate (15), with lenses of marble (high limestone) and soapstone (upland granite or slate). Deposits as veins, laid
island by island, the rarest first, a tile one deposit: gold (peaks, chance 0.08), tin (upland granite, 0.25), lead and silver
(uplands, 0.3), copper (uplands, 0.25), iron (uplands, 0.45), amber (beaches, 0.1), salt springs (inland limestone or sandstone, 0.1,
endless), flint (low limestone, 0.5), bog iron (marsh and lake shores, 0.7), peat (marsh, 0.8), clay (low and damp, 0.9). Amounts in
loads are placeholders "until the goods that use them" (gold 10 to 80 a tile, tin 80 to 400, lead-silver 100 to 500, copper 150 to
800, iron 200 to 1,200; a few first tiles endless).

**What the simulation reads.**
- `sim/prospect` (VB-358): the five upland ores are hidden; a finished job within 2 tiles of a vein finds it with chance hours / 60,
  and the village knows it (`st.finds`). Nothing reads a find but the views and the log.
- `sim/dig` (VB-359, VB-367): clay, bog iron and peat are dug, a load a trip, 3 hours, within 12 steps, carried home; clay and bog
  iron only to a stock of 4 loads, since nothing uses them. Bog iron grows back a load a year. The hidden ores, flint, amber and salt
  are named in its header as waiting.
- `sim/building` (VB-332): stone picked from stony meadows or quarried on hills or mountains, for a stone foundation; the heap keeps
  no kind.
- `sim/quern` (VB-009): a quern of sandstone or granite, taken from the bedrock under the green (not from where the stone was
  quarried), wears out in 20 years.
- Nothing reads copper, tin, lead-silver, gold, flint, amber, slate, marble or soapstone; limestone only as stone for a foundation.

**What the design and the backlog say.**

## 4. The metals

### 4.1 Bronze, and copper on its own

History (medieval Nordic, 1100 to 1500). Iron made the tools; copper alloys made what iron could not: cooking cauldrons and kettles
(the three-legged cast *malmgryta* is one of the commonest finds of medieval Swedish households), brewing and dairy kettles of sheet
copper, buckles, brooches, scale weights, candlesticks, and the church's bells, cast in a pit beside the church by travelling bell
founders. Copper came from a few great mines (Falun, worked from about the year 1000 and big from the 13th century; I am unsure of
the earliest date), tin by sea from England and later Saxony. So bronze never came first: the Bronze Age was two thousand years
gone, and a medieval village's iron was its own while its bronze came from far off.

What it is for in the game, beside iron (decided by 7 and 9, the shape in choice 2):
- **Copper alone, the kettle.** A sheet-copper kettle is the household's brewing and dairy vessel. Its need: ale (slice 4: 10
  rations of grain and 4 hours a barrel) is brewed in fewer hours, or more barrels a brewing, with a kettle; later, cheese and dyeing.
  It wears through in about 30 years and is patched with copper by a smith, so copper has a small recurring sink. Copper alone needs
  no second place, so a copper village has a good of its own.
- **Bronze, copper and tin, the cast pot and the bell.** A cast bronze pot lasts generations: it is a dowry good and an heirloom,
  inherited as movable worth (VB-276, the heirs' shares). The bell is the village's: its meeting decides to have one, the alderman
  orders it (principle 16), a caster casts it in a pit by the green, and it gives the village standing at the gathering. Its sink is
  that a bell cracks (rarely) and a pot is recast; the recurring sink is weak, and that is the point of choice 2.
- **Whether it comes first.** No: iron first (VB-701, with the fair, from the bog), bronze after mining (VB-733) and smelting
  (VB-735). Reason: history, and iron is a need (tools that wear) where bronze is plenty and standing (principle 1: the feast binds,
  and a pot or a bell is how plenty shows).
- **The four tests.** Copper: place, yes (uplands, found); sink, the kettle wears; decision, a kettle or a heifer; crossing, yes.
  Tin: place, the rarest ore; sink, only through bronze; decision, the caster's; crossing, always. Bronze: all four, the sink weak.
- **Two places, one good.** Bronze is cast by whoever holds both metals; copper and tin each move by load to the caster (nothing
  teleports). Since tin is almost never in reach (section 3), on most maps bronze waits for a boat (slice 10) or the ship; choice 3
  asks whether that is too scarce.
- **Pewter** (tin and lead) was a late medieval town craft; it is a second use of tin, and waits for the town (slice 9). Not a key
  kind before then (7).

### 4.2 Silver and gold

History. Before and well after the first Nordic coins (about 1000; steady coinage in Sweden from the 13th century), silver went by
weight: hack silver weighed on folding scales, the provincial laws' fines and wergild counted in marks and öre of silver, dowries and
morning gifts given in silver, ornaments worn as a household's worth, and hoards buried and often never recovered (Gotland's hundreds
of hoards). Silver came with lead, from the same ore (Sala, from the 15th century; earlier mostly from abroad). Gold was marginal in
the north: finger rings, the church's plate.

What a household does with them before coin (decided by 1, 8 and 13; the shape in choice 4):
- **Keeps.** Silver is the one good that neither spoils nor wears: a household turns a surplus it cannot eat into worth that waits.
  This is also its danger to principle 1 (the feast should be where plenty goes): so silver is got only from someone who has it, by a
  swap at its customary worth, and the head's target (VB-698) for silver is "what I mean to give", not "all I can".
- **Gives.** The dowry and the morning gift (VB-276) carried in silver, which goes with the bride (a cow walks, a chest is carried);
  a gift to bind a follower (VB-708's big men); a fine paid (slice 5's cases). Each is a crossing between households.
- **Wears.** A brooch or a ring shown on the pawn as an icon (principle 15), a small term of standing with a cap, as the well-kept
  house's is.
- **Loses.** The recurring sink: a hoard is buried by a pawn (an act, at its house); where it lies is the pawn's knowledge, passed to
  the heir at death if the pawn was able to tell, else lost with it. The silver stays on the tile (nothing vanishes) and may be found
  again by work there, as a vein is (sim/prospect's chance). Grounded in Gotland's hoards; a good story for the record.
- **The ship's silver (VB-188).** The ship brings silver and, with an established Crown, coin: the island's own silver is then what
  the Crown mints first, and a lead-silver village is worth a town (In the ground: "an endless source worth a town"). This plan only
  asks that VB-188 read the island's silver as a stock already in households, not start from none.
- **Gold.** The same rows at about ten times silver's worth by weight; rings as a morning gift. Found on few maps (section 3); kept
  as a key kind because Kim asked for it, and it costs only a row (choice 7).
- **The four tests.** Silver: place, yes (uplands, found, 15 to 19% of villages within a day); sink, buried and lost, given away;
  decision, a head chooses silver or a feast, a dowry in silver or in cattle; crossing, its main use. Gold: the same, rarer. Lead:
  place, with silver; sink, none until the church's roofs and windows (slice 8); decision, none: lead is a by-product that waits.

### 4.3 The veins: how one is mined

History. Medieval Swedish mining (Bergslagen, Falun) was done by peasant miners (*bergsmän*) who farmed as well, in open pits and
shallow shafts, breaking the rock by fire-setting: a fire built against the face, the rock cracked as it cooled, then hammer and wedge;
it ate wood by the cartload. The ore was roasted and smelted near the mine in a small furnace (the *hytta*; Lapphyttan, about 1200, is
the oldest known blast furnace), owned in shares by the farms that worked it. So: hands first, a pit, wood; the furnace is the
facility.

Decided here (by 4, 9, 16 and the hard rule):
- **A pawn digs, as clay is dug** (principle 4: copper is mined as iron is). A found vein (`sim/prospect` knows) on the household's
  land, its village's outland or the wilderness (sim/dig's `mayDig`) can be dug a load a trip. No facility for an open pit: the tile's
  loads left are the pit, and it gets harder as it goes down (hours a load rise as the tile's loads fall below half). The job takes
  firewood from the household's pile for the fire-setting: proposed 3 hours and 1 load of wood for a load of rock ore at the surface,
  6 hours and 1.5 loads below half (numbers Proposed, to be found in the task's five short runs).
- **Ore is heavy, so smelting is near the mine.** A load of ore carried home 12 steps is a trip; a farm 30 steps off carries a bloom,
  not ore. This is principle 9's mechanism: the village near the vein smelts and trades metal, the cost of carrying makes it so.
- **The furnace is a facility** (VB-361), built by a household or by the village's meeting as a commons work day (principle 16: the
  alderman carries out what the meeting decided; each hour is a pawn's). Rock iron goes to the bloomery (VB-364) at a better yield than
  bog ore; copper and lead-silver need the furnace (VB-735).
- **Who owns the ore:** whoever dug it, as with clay. Shares in a mine (the *bergslag*) are a practice for later; choice 5 asks whether
  they come first.
- **Prospecting as work** (In the ground: "waits for the first good that needs ore"): it comes now. A head that wants ore, and knows
  none, sends a pawn to the uplands for a day (a verb; the chance is `sim/prospect`'s, hours / 60 within 2 tiles).
- **Amounts.** In the ground says a village's vein should last about three generations once a year's use is named. At the rates
  proposed (a mining household 10 to 20 loads a year), a vein of 2 to 5 tiles at today's amounts (copper 150 to 800 a tile) lasts
  20 to 200 years: about right, to be checked by the task.

Decided here (by 7, 8, 9):
- **Granite needs no new use:** it is the ground everyone has; it fails the crossing test by design, and that is its role (the stone
  a village without better uses).
- **Sandstone** keeps its Decided uses; a grindstone (a wheel, for edges) joins the tools (VB-738) as sandstone's second sink.
- **Limestone:** lime as decided (VB-368); limewash as a recurring upkeep of the stone house (a load of lime in 5 years) gives lime
  a sink after the house is built. Proposed, as a line in VB-370's numbers, not a new system.
- **Slate** gets the whetstone (choice 6) beside the roof: the roof is once a house, the whetstone recurs with the tools.
- **Soapstone** gets the vessel now (VB-739), as a kind of VB-366's pot (principle 4: a new kind of pot is made as pots are): it
  breaks a twentieth a year where fired clay breaks a quarter, so a soapstone village has a good every household wants. Carving and
  the church stay slice 8. Soapstone moulds are where bronze is cast (VB-736), a second tie.
- **Marble** waits for the church (slice 8): no decision before it. **Amber** waits for the ship and the church: a gift good
  before them is possible (choice 8), but it adds a good with no need. **Flint** waits, with the reason that a household keeps its
  fire and needs no decision about a strike-a-light; it can come as a fair good when fire-lighting matters (a journey, a camp), and a
  map colour with that reason is allowed by the task's goal.

## 6. Decisions made without Kim, with reasons

- D1. Iron comes before bronze (4.1): iron is a need and is already pulled forward (VB-701); history.
- D2. Copper has a use of its own (the kettle), so a copper village does not wait for tin (principle 9: each place a good; 7: a
  decision, a kettle or an animal).
- D3. A vein is dug by hands as clay is, a load a trip, with wood for fire-setting; no facility for the pit (principle 4; history).
  Close call: a mine could be a facility from the start (VB-361); I chose the pit as the tile so that mining needs only sim/dig and
  the wood, and the furnace is the one facility.
- D4. Smelting near the mine; the ore is not carted far (principle 9 by the cost of carrying; the hard rule).
- D5. Silver's recurring sink is the hoard lost with its keeper (nothing vanishes: it stays on the tile and can be found). Principle
  candidate: "a good that does not wear needs a way to leave play that is a process: given, buried, lost with knowledge".
- D6. Soapstone vessels as a kind of pot (principle 4), the church's carving left to slice 8.
- D7. Quarrying limestone and sandstone stays on hills and mountains for now; widening it (any tile of the rock that is not field or
  meadow) is noted for VB-368, which needs limestone, to decide with its numbers.
- D8. The quern should read the rock of the stone it was hewn from (the heap by kind, VB-332's note), not the green's; noted for the
  task that gives the heap kinds (VB-738 carries it, since whetstones need kinds too).
- D9. Lead and pewter wait (7: not key kinds before the town and the church).
- D10. Flint and marble wait, with reasons (5).

## 8. Design choices for Kim

1. **When do the island's own metals enter?** In the ground (Decided, 11 Nov) left copper, tin, lead, silver and gold for slice 8,
   and the ship is now the last slice. (a) They wait for the ship, as decided. (b) They enter in slice 6's second half as goods of
   place: mined from found veins, smelted, made into kettles, pots and silver for dowries; the bell and coin still wait for the church
   and the Crown. (c) They enter with the town (slice 9), where the smith and the caster can live by their craft. **Proposed: (b).**
   Kim asked for them to be included, and with the ship last, (a) leaves the found veins as map colour for most of the game.
2. **What is bronze for beside iron?** (a) Household goods: a copper kettle for brewing and dairy, and a cast bronze pot that is a
   dowry and an heirloom. (b) Only the village's bell and the church's vessels, for standing. (c) Both: the kettle and pot for
   households, the bell for the village's meeting to decide. **Proposed: (c),** with iron first in any case. A household decision
   and a village one, each historical; the household sink is what makes copper recur.
3. **Is tin too scarce?** Within a day's walk of 13 of 189 small and 1 of 81 medium villages; copper and tin both on the same small
   map on 3 of 16. (a) Keep it: bronze is a good of the few maps and otherwise of boats and the ship (principle 9 at its hardest).
   (b) Raise tin's chance (0.25 to about 0.5) and let it lie in granite hills, not only the uplands. (c) Add stream tin: a placer
   deposit in rivers below a tin vein, found by working the river. **Proposed: (a),** with copper useful alone (the kettle), so
   bronze's scarcity costs no village a need. If Kim wants bronze before the boats on most maps, (b) is the one-line change.
4. **Silver before coin: a gift, or a means of exchange?** (a) A good of worth only: kept, given (dowry, morning gift, to a follower,
   a fine), worn for standing, buried; at a swap it is a good like any other, at its customary worth. (b) Also taken by weight as
   change at the fair, so a swap that lacks change can close (VB-302 counts those); a step to coin. (c) It waits for the ship and
   the Crown. **Proposed: (a),** and (b) only as a norm the fair learns (target-v2 5.3), not a rule: barter until coin is Decided
   (10 Nov), and silver as change is coin by another name.
5. **Who works a mine?** (a) Any household digs a found vein on its land, the outland or the wilderness, a load a trip, and owns
   what it dug (as clay). (b) The village's meeting opens it as commons, worked on work days, the metal to the village store. (c)
   A mine in shares (the *bergslag*): those who work it hold shares, and the furnace is theirs together. **Proposed: (a) first,
   (c) later as a practice.** Principle 4 (copper mined as iron is) and 16 (the digger chose it); shares need slice 5's bodies.
6. **Whetstones of slate?** In the ground names sandstone for querns, whetstones and grindstones; historically the hand whetstone
   was schist (Eidsborg, traded across the north) and the grindstone sandstone. (a) Whetstones of slate (as schist), grindstones of
   sandstone. (b) Both of sandstone, as written. (c) Either rock. **Proposed: (a).** It gives slate a recurring sink beside the
   roof and a second stone a trade; the mechanism is as decided, only the source moves.
7. **Gold.** Found on 2 small maps of 16, on no medium map measured. (a) The same rows as silver at about ten times its worth (rings,
   the morning gift). (b) Not mined by islanders (marginal in the north); it comes only by ship. (c) Folded into silver as one
   precious metal. **Proposed: (a).** Kim asked for it; it costs a row, and on a map that has it it is a story.
8. **Stones and amber for standing before the church.** (a) Marble, soapstone carving and amber wait for the church (slice 8), as
   decided; soapstone gets the vessel now (VB-739). (b) Also a memorial stone at a holder's funeral, raised by the heir for standing
   (the runestone and grave-slab custom), finer in marble or limestone, before the church. (c) Also amber beads as a gift good now.
   **Proposed: (a).** (b) is attractive and historical but a new act and a new sink; it fits the church's plan better.

## 9. What this draft does not settle

- The numbers of every recipe (hours, loads, wear) are Proposed from general reading, not checked by search; each task finds its
  own in five short runs.
- The scarcity is of a populous start; a run's villages that settle later, nearer the hills, are not counted. VB-740 would show it at
  any year.
- Whether the western shores of a Nordic setting had amber at all (it is Baltic); the generator places it on any sandy shore.
- Falun's earliest working, and how long a soapstone vessel lasted in use: unsure.
