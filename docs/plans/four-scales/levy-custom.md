# The custom of the levy, grounded: what the sources say about whose hours were whose (1300 to 1700)

25 November 2026. The research pass that Kim's answer 12 asks for (`README.md` of this folder, section 6): the values of the custom
table in section 3.3 ("The custom, by position and season"), and of the outer levies, set against historical sources. Nothing here is
decided: it is the evidence, a table that follows from it, and the arithmetic between them, for the measurement to start from. Each
value carries a mark: **source** (a scholarly source or a legal text says it), **inferred** (arithmetic on sourced facts, or a
pattern the sources show without giving the number), **guess** (no source found). Sources are listed at the end of each topic.

## 2. Translation to the game's units

**A game day is a real week; its 12 hours are the week's working capacity.** The game's 06:00 to 18:00 is fixed in every season
(`design.md`, 3. Time and calendar), so it cannot show a longer summer day or a dark winter one. I take the 12 hours to stand for
all of a real week's working time, Sundays included, so that one real day is 12 ÷ 7 = **1.71 game hours** of each game day. A day
of rest is then own time spread over the game day as hours, as the design wants, and a whole real week (a free week at the term,
Christmas) is a **whole game day**.

**A season is 13 real weeks**: 91 days, 13 Sundays, 78 weekdays.

**Holy days by season** (weekdays only; Lutheran Sweden-Finland, 1571 to 1772, after the church order of 1571 and the church law
of 1686, chapter 14; the apostles' days and the rogation days counted as half-days, since work was allowed after the service; each
movable or fixed feast weighted by its chance, 6 in 7, of falling on a weekday; one prayer day, böndag, a season):

| Season | Feasts | Weekday day-equivalents `H` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spring | Annunciation; Maundy Thursday, Good Friday, Easter's 2nd to 4th days; Ascension; the 3 rogation days (half); part of Pentecost; 2 apostles (half); a prayer day | about 12 |
| Summer | the rest of Pentecost; St John (Midsummer); the Visitation; about 4 apostles (half); a prayer day | about 5.4 |
| Autumn | Michaelmas; All Saints; about 4 apostles (half); a prayer day | about 4.4 |
| Winter | Christmas's 4 days; New Year; Epiphany; Candlemas; about 3 apostles (half); a prayer day | about 8.3 |

The year comes to about 30 weekday holy days and 52 Sundays, about 82 days, which matches the official count of about 80 for the
early 16th century (SOU 1947:27).

**The ceiling.** If a body claims every weekday whole and no rest day, its share is `(78 − H) ÷ 91`, times 12. To that I add the
beasts on rest days: feeding and milking do not stop on a Sunday, and I count them as a quarter of a rest day (3 of 12 hours), a
guess, for those on the byre's rota. So the ceiling is `12 × ((78 − H) + 0.25 × (13 + H)) ÷ 91`:

| | Spring | Summer | Autumn | Winter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weekdays claimed, `12 × (78 − H) ÷ 91` | 8.7 | 9.6 | 9.7 | 9.2 |
| Chores on rest days | +0.8 | +0.6 | +0.6 | +0.7 |
| **Ceiling** | **9.5** | **10.2** | **10.3** | **9.9** |
| Rounded for the table | 9.5 | 10 | 10.5 (holy half-days worked at harvest, inferred) | 9.5 (the Christmas peace to St Knut, custom) |

Other calendars move the ceiling: **Catholic, before the 1530s**, 30 to 50 weekday holy days (Denmark about 50 at the end of the
15th century; Sweden about 30 by the official count; manors in England from a handful to about 30), so the ceiling falls by up to
0.7 hours, most in winter and spring. **Lutheran Denmark-Norway, 1539 to 1770**: 16 holy days, so the ceiling rises by about 0.5.
**Denmark after 1770, Sweden after 1772**: 7 in Denmark, about 20 fewer in Sweden, so the ceiling rises by a further 0.3 to 0.5.

**The working day itself is the master's.** A servant was hired by the year to the household's work; I count the whole working day
as levied, meal breaks included (they are taken at the master's table and are not free to spend). Evenings: the game's day has no
evening, and the 12 hours already stand for the whole week's working time, so a servant's evening, where it was free, is part of the
rest-day hours, not added to them.

**Outer levies.** A real labour day (dagsverke, hoveri day) is 1.71 game hours. The design books an outer day at 8 game hours, so
one game adult-day is 8 ÷ 1.71 = **4.7 real days**: real days a year ÷ 4.7 = game adult-days a year. A day a week for a year (52) is
11 game adult-days; three days a week in a season (39) are 8. Lords' days were owed by the farm, after its land (mantal in Sweden,
hartkorn in Denmark), not by its adults.

**A notice.** The Danish corvée regulation of 1791 gave the tenant notice the evening before. In the game that is less than a day:
notice 0. The design's 3 days of notice are 3 real weeks.

## 3. Findings by topic

### 3.1 Servants

**Sweden-Finland.** Servants' acts (legostadgor, later tjänstehjonsstadgor) of 1664, 1686, 1723, 1739, 1805 and 1833, in force
until 1926 (Högman). Everyone without a farm or a burgher's trade had to take service by the year as a farmhand (dräng) or a maid
(piga) (the 1739 act, after Borenberg 2023); how far that was enforced is unclear, and authorities complained it was ignored. The
term was a year: before the 1833 act, from Michaelmas (29 September); notice could be given between Olof's mass and Lars's mass
(29 July to 10 August) under the 1739 act; from 1833 the moving day was 24 October, and the week after it, the free week
(frivecka, slankvecka), let servants take new places (Högman; Chydenius edition). The master's authority (husbondevälde) covered
the servant's person: chastisement (husaga) was lawful until 1858 for all, until 1920 for the young (Högman). Lundh: once the
contract was made, "a relation of subordination and superiority was also established" (quoted in Lyngholm 2023). The 1723 and 1739
acts set wage tariffs in money and kind, and fined a master who paid above them (Chydenius edition). There were more maids than
farmhands on 18th and 19th century farms; farms took servants while their own children were small (Högman).

I could not read the acts themselves on hours, Sundays or a servant's work for himself (section 5).

**Denmark.** Servants could change places only on 1 May or 1 November, with twelve weeks' notice before witnesses (the 1791 police
regulation; Lyngholm 2023). Before 1770 there were the **skulteuger**: "two or three weeks around Easter and November in which
servants and farmhands were not required to work and were free to change their jobs and cultivate their own patch of land
sometimes received as payment in kind from their employer known as lønnesæd". In 1770 they were abolished and the change dates moved
to 31 December and 30 June, "periods that were unsuitable for working the land" (Jensen, Radu and Sharp 2019, after Kjærgaard
1994). The Danish Code of 1683 (6-5-5) let the head of the household chastise "his children and servants with a cane or rod";
servants were part of the household, and a manor's people part of the lord's (Lyngholm 2023). A male servant's wage in the late
1700s was 9 to 28 rigsdaler a year, in money and kind (ibid.).

**Norway.** Compulsory service entered Christian V's Norwegian Law of 1687 and stood for nearly 200 years. Contracts ran six months
or a year; the moving days were 14 April and 14 October, with seven weeks' notice. About three quarters of farms had a servant,
usually one girl. Servants started at about 12, the poorest at 6 or 7. About half the wage was paid in clothes (58% for both
sexes in one study). One survey says servants worked 15 to 16 hours a day, without a source. A law of 1754 forbade a farmer to keep
more than one son and one daughter at home: the others had to serve elsewhere (Miscali, after Sogner, Bakke, Myrheim).

**The continent.** England's statute of 1495 set field labourers' hours (below, 3.7); servants in husbandry were hired by the year.
Denmark's and Sweden's laws were of the same kind: a year's contract, the household's discipline, and a duty to serve laid on the
landless.

**What this says about own time.** In every one of these laws the servant's own time comes as **whole days**: Sundays and holy
days, and the days around the term (a week in Sweden, two or three weeks twice a year in Denmark before 1770). The Danish servant's
lønnesæd, a patch sown for him as part of his wage, was worked in his skulteuger: an own plot, worked in own days, granted by the
contract.

Sources: Hans Högman, "The Agricultural Society" (hhogman.se/agricultural-society.htm); P. Borenberg, "Pigor i sämre hushåll",
*Sjuttonhundratal* 2023 (septentrio.uit.no/index.php/1700/article/view/6894); Chydenius, *Kootut teokset*, commentary on the 1739
act (kootutteokset.chydenius.fi); D. K. Lyngholm, "Absolute Obedience: Servants and Masters on Danish Estates in the Nineteenth
Century", *International Review of Social History* 68 (2023), 177–195, doi:10.1017/S0020859022000918; P. S. Jensen, C. V. Radu
and P. R. Sharp, "Days Worked and Seasonality Patterns of Work in Eighteenth Century Denmark", SDU Discussion Paper 10/2019
(sdu.dk), citing T. Kjærgaard, *The Danish Revolution 1500–1800* (1994); M. Miscali, "¿Qué pasó en el Norte? Sirvientes y
trabajadores rurales en Noruega", *Mundo Agrario* (mundoagrario.unlp.edu.ar, article MAe064); H. Østhus and C. Uppenberg, "Servants

### 3.2 Sons and daughters at home

The evidence here is thin, and almost all indirect.

- **Adult children at home were the farm's hands in place of servants.** Farms hired servants when their own children were small and
  fewer once the children could work (Högman). The servant system has been read as the peasant economy's way of moving labour
  between households whose children did not match their land (Dribe and Lundh).
- **The law pushed the surplus out.** Norway's law of 1754: one son and one daughter at home, the rest into service (Miscali).
  Sweden's acts made everyone without land serve, and children at home were the exemption; the 1833 act still had rules on parents'
  use of their children's labour at home (Riksdag bill 1926:183, after search results; not read).
- **Leaving home.** In 19th century Scania, peasants' children left home to serve elsewhere in response to the household's needs and
  the harvests, more than the landless' children did, whose leaving was earlier and more fixed (Dribe 2000).
- **Heir and younger sons.** No source I found distinguishes an heir's hours from a younger son's at home. The difference the
  sources show is of fate: the heir stayed; a younger son went into service, to a croft, or to the sea or the army.
- **Gender.** Women's work was the house, the byre (milking, the small beasts), and the textiles; spinning was "uniformly described as
  women's work", done "by all women of the household: mistress, maids, girls, and older women", in the evenings, "only when
  everything else for the day was finished" (Uppenberg and Nilsson 2023, from ethnological questionnaires, 19th century). Men
  carted, threshed, ploughed; haymaking and the harvest took everyone, "men, women, and children worked together in the fields,
  including servants". Women's labour on the estates was concentrated from May to October; on one estate no women's days were
  registered in winter at all (ibid.).
- **Own earnings.** I found no source on sons' or daughters' own plots or animals in the period. A daughter's chest (textiles for her
  marriage) is well known from ethnology but I found no source on whose hours made it; an elder's contract could include "the right
  to sow a certain amount of flax" (Lundh and Olsson 2002), and a servant's wage a sown patch (Denmark), so a plot of one's own was a
  known form of payment for labour.

Sources: Högman; Miscali; M. Dribe, *Leaving Home in a Peasant Society* (2000), abstract (lup.lub.lu.se/record/19689); C.
Uppenberg and M. Nilsson, "The crofter is a woman: Gender division of labour in rural semi-landless households, Sweden 1800–1900",
Lund Papers in Economic History 2023:253 (lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/158847957/LUPEH_253.pdf).

### 3.3 Retirement contracts

**Sweden.** Undantag (the pension) and födoråd (the deliveries) were "well established in Sweden in the Middle Ages", written
contracts later. The retired gave up the farm "in exchange for free board and lodging", in meals at the table or in kind (grain,
meat, milk, fodder and grazing for cows or sheep, a potato plot, a garden plot, wood or peat), and could keep "the right to farm
certain plots of land, fish in the property's waters". Retirement meant "that old people were eased out of the regular work force
and were spared heavy and arduous tasks". Most contracts had two phases: first the pensioner farmed his own bit of land, then he
became dependent. And: "**The majority of contracts did not specify service in return for subsistence**"; for the well-off within
the family "a continued interest in the running of the estate" may be assumed; small farmers and crofters with small pensions had to
work for others. The age at retirement was about 60 for men, somewhat less for women (Lundh and Olsson 2002, 19th century Scania,
with the literature on freeholders). Widows' contracts kept light work, milking or weaving (a *Continuity and Change* abstract, after
search results). Högman: the undantag cottage (undantagsstuga); the right outlived a sale of the farm.

**Norway** (kår, føderåd): a private agreement, never set by law, written contracts usual from the 1600s (lokalhistoriewiki, after
search results). **Austria and Germany** (Ausgedinge, Altenteil, Leibzucht): the duty of support lay on the holding, not the
children, so it survived a sale (a Lower Austrian study, 1780–1849); I found no source on work owed by the retired.

So the contract's levy on an elder is **nil**: what an elder works is his own plot (own hours) and what he gives.

Sources: C. Lundh and M. Olsson, "The institution of retirement on Scanian estates in the nineteenth century", *Continuity and
Change* 17:3 (2002), 373–403, doi:10.1017/S0268416002004393; Högman.

### 3.4 Holy days and rest

- **Sweden.** The medieval festal year held "over a hundred festa fori"; at the start of the 16th century the feasts came to about
  80 days, about 50 of them Sundays. The church order of 1571 kept 4 days each at Christmas, Easter and Pentecost, New Year,
  Epiphany, Maundy Thursday, Good Friday, Ascension, the Transfiguration, three Marian days, St John, Michaelmas, All Saints and the
  apostles' days. The church law of 1686 (chapter 14) kept them but for the Transfiguration, and made the apostles' days half-holy:
  after the service people might "driva deras handel och vandel samt annat lovligt arbete" (do their trade and other lawful work).
  Days cut in 1741 came back in 1743. The ordinance of 4 November 1772 abolished from 1773 the 3rd and 4th days of the three great
  feasts, the Visitation, Maundy Thursday, the apostles' days and the rogation days, and moved Candlemas, Michaelmas and All Saints
  to the following Sunday (SOU 1947:27). Sweden kept "almost twice as many" holy days as Denmark at the Reformation; the 1772
  reform cut about 20 (Malmstedt, in The Local 2024). Peasants defended their holy days, and many abolished days (St Olof's) went on
  being kept (Malmstedt 2014; Kuha 2016 on Savo, Finland, to 1710).
- **Denmark-Norway.** About 50 holy days at the end of the 15th century; the church ordinance of 1537/39 kept 16 and Sundays; Bishop
  Palladius told the peasants to work on the "monks' days": "God has commanded you to work, you day-thief, not to sit down and drink
  ale!". The ordinance of 26 October 1770 abolished nine more, leaving seven (Jensen, Radu and Sharp 2019, after Rørdam and
  Kjærgaard), among them Epiphany, Candlemas, St John's, Michaelmas, All Saints and the third days of the great feasts.
- **England, for comparison.** Holy days observed on the manors of Ramsey Abbey ranged "from a handful of religious festivals a year
  to an upper number of around 30" (Brown and Owen, Durham, 2025).
- **The season.** The holy days fall thickest at Christmas and from Easter to Pentecost, and thinnest at hay and harvest (section 2).
  Whether harvest work was allowed on them: the apostles' half-days allowed work after the service from 1686; I found no source for
  a general harvest exemption, and mark the autumn's half-hour for it as inferred.

Sources: SOU 1947:27 (lagen.nu/sou/1947:27), historical overview of holy days; The Local, "Why is Maundy Thursday a holiday in
Denmark and Norway but not Sweden?" (2024), quoting G. Malmstedt; G. Malmstedt, "In Defence of Holy Days: The Peasantry's
Opposition to the Reduction of Holy Days in Early Modern Sweden" (2014), and *Helgdagsreduktionen* (1994), not read; M. Kuha,
*Pyhäpäivien vietto varhaismodernin ajan Savossa* (Jyväskylä 2016); Jensen, Radu and Sharp 2019; Durham University news, 26
November 2025, on A. Brown and G. Owen, doi:10.1017/jbr.2025.10130.

### 3.5 Village labour duties

- **Roads.** From the provincial laws (Hälsingelagen, about 1320) and Magnus Eriksson's law of the 1350s, landowning peasants built
  and kept the public roads and bridges. Each road was divided into **road lots** (väglotter), assigned to the farms "in proportion to
  their mantal-set land", marked by stones with the farm's name; a farm could hold lots anywhere in the parish, and farms with no
  road over their land still had lots. Roads were kept "twice a year, usually before the spring sowing and after the autumn harvest",
  and inspected each year (vägsyn); farmers often gravelled just before the inspection. The 1734 code kept the duty; it lay on the
  mantal-set land, in kind, until the road act of 1891 (Högman, road history; Riksdag papers). The lot was the farm's own to keep in
  its own time before the inspection: the levied named the day.
- **Fences.** Each farm kept its own stretch of the village's fence, by its share of the fields, under the provincial laws and the
  1734 code (an SLU report, after search results); mid-17th century maps show whole "fence-organisations" (*Journal of Historical
  Geography*, 2023, after search results).
- **By-laws.** Village by-laws (byordningar) set the village's meeting, its ålderman, fences, ditches, roads and grazing; the Crown
  sent a model ordinance in 37 points in 1742 for villages to adapt (Riksdag papers and a study of Södermanland, after search
  results). Breach was fined at the meeting.
- **Days.** I found **no count of days per farm a year** for any of these. The table's 7 to 22 real days (1.5 to 4.7 game adult-days)
  is a guess, from two road windows of one to three days, a fence of a few days in spring, and a few days of other common work.

Sources: Hans Högman, "Road history" (hhogman.se/road-history-swe.htm); Byggningabalk 1736 (lagen.nu/1736:0123_1); search-result
summaries of an SLU report on fences and of Kallio (Jyväskylä) on the 1742 by-law in Ostrobothnia.

### 3.6 Labour dues to lords

- **Denmark.** The tenant (fæstebonde) of the late Middle Ages was an "ugedagstjener", a weekday servant: about a day a week. From
  the early 1500s hoveri had no fixed limit; it grew through the 1600s and 1700s and by the end of the 1700s reached "nearly every day
  of the week" on some estates; in the 1780s it was worth four times the land rent. Kinds of day: spanddage (with cart and team),
  pløjedage (with plough and team), gangdage (one person on foot); a fixed area (hovmål); rotation between farms (omgangshoveri) or all
  together (flængshoveri). Outside the peaks a tenant could send a farmhand in his place; a cottager rarely could. Ordinances: 1769
  (written regulations for every estate), 1771 (by hartkorn, with receipts, and the hours of 3.7), 1773 (open again), 1799 (fixed at
  the level reached, not lowered). The 1791 regulation: notice the evening before, but the lord might "summon them immediately with
  unexpected work"; ten hours a day in the field; punishments for "carelessness, being late for work, or not turning up". Commutation
  to money by agreement from the 1830s; by law on demand from 1850; about half the tenanted land free of hoveri by 1835 (lex.dk;
  danskeherregaarde.dk; Lyngholm 2023).
- **Sweden, Scania.** On the frälse estates hoveri was "all work and errands asked on the lordship's behalf", "at the first demand",
  in person or by sending "such capable and reliable folk as can do the work" (Knutstorp's standard contracts, 1820 to 1860). At
  Bergsjöholm in 1789 the tenants owed "daily labour days ... for large parts of the year", and each of the larger farms must have kept
  at least one farmhand only to do the hoveri. Cottagers had their days written in: 82 or 104 a year at Dufeke, 52 to 132 at
  Knutstorp. Many estates **divided the demesne's fields into lots** (indelt hoveri), each farm working its strips from ploughing to
  harvest, the lot's size set by a "hoveri number" between a quarter and one, related to but not the same as mantal. A contemporary
  (1781): "no idleness finds room, since each is eager to finish what falls to him, as he is then free". Peasants on Crown manors
  won in court against added work (Lindholmen, on a contract of 1688, 1786); frälse peasants lost every case (Olsson and Svensson
  1999). Peasants' main contribution to the landlord became corvée labour (Olsson 2006).
- **Sweden, the central provinces.** At Esplunda (1782): two to three days a week by farm size from mid-March "until the hay harvest
  begins, as commonly happens at the start of July"; then each farm's lots of meadow and field in the harvest; from late August the
  usual days again; from the end of October and through the winter one day a week for all (Magnusson, after Olsson and Svensson 1999).
  The nobility's privileges of 1723 freed its tenants of the Crown's labour days (§ 9).
- **Sweden-Finland, crofters.** In 19th century contracts men owed on average 103 days a year, women 13 (where days were set);
  estates' averages ran from 66 to 148, single crofts from 36 to 312; women's days were set "per summer season" or for a task ("xx
  days during haymaking or with flax") and worked May to October; crofts without days paid cash; extra days at the lord's call were
  under a tenth of the set ones. Since the harvest "had to be done during a few weeks or even a few critical days ... the landowners'
  fields were prioritised", and the crofters' own crops were "destroyed by rain the next day" (Uppenberg and Nilsson 2023). At its
  heaviest, Swedish and Finnish peasants' labour service "may have been on a par with the corvée in the early modern Baltic
  provinces" (Rahikainen 2020).
- **The continent.** Bohemia: before 1680 robot was unlimited, "whenever the manor demanded it"; the patent of 28 June 1680 capped it
  at three days a week, except harvest, haymaking and draining the fish-ponds (Hungarian and Czech studies, after search results;
  Cerman's edition, 2019). South-west Germany: at the end of the Middle Ages the dues were "almost everywhere ... measured and very
  light"; they peaked between the later 16th century and the Thirty Years' War; at Bünzwangen (Württemberg) they rose to 72 days for
  each holder by the early 19th century (Grenzboten, after search results). East of the Elbe, full Gutsherrschaft meant up to six days
  a week a household, and two or three a week was the point at which a farm had to keep a team for the dues alone (a Münster lecture
  script, after search results). England, about 1300: customary tenants owed "between a day and three days per week" by holding size;
  leave for sickness ranged from none to a year and a day (Brown and Owen 2025).

Sources: lex.dk, "Hoveri" (C. Bjørn, P. Grau Møller); danskeherregaarde.dk, "Godsets arbejdskraft"; Lyngholm 2023; M. Olsson and

### 3.7 The working day by season

- **Denmark, 1771** (the hoveri ordinance): November to February, 8:00 to 16:00 with an hour's break (7 hours); March, April,
  September and October, 7:00 to 18:00 with two hours' rest (9); May to August, 6:00 to 19:00 with two hours' break (11). In the
  1790s some manors set sunrise to sunset in winter and 5:00 to 18:00 or 19:00 in summer, with no breaks from September to April:
  "during the summer the working day was between 12 and 14 hours while during the winter the working day lasted throughout the
  daylight hours, which are a little over seven hours on the shortest day". Between 1500 and 1800 the working day lengthened by three
  or four hours and the week by one or two days (Kjærgaard, in Jensen, Radu and Sharp 2019). Day labourers in 18th century Denmark
  worked about 5.6 days a week in full employment, but only about 184 days a year, for want of winter work (ibid.).
- **Norway**: servants 15 to 16 hours a day in the 1700s and 1800s (Miscali, unsourced).
- **England**: the statute of 1495 set March to September at 5:00 to 19:00 or 20:00 and winter at 5:00 to dark, rarely enforced;
  court depositions show work "generally concentrated between 8am and 4pm all year round" (Hailwood 2016).
- **Sweden**: I found no source on statutory or customary hours; the acts are said to regulate them (Högman).

The ratio of the Danish hours, 7 : 9 : 11 for winter, spring and autumn, summer, is the best grounded seasonal shape of a day's work.
In the game's fixed day it cannot shorten the hours; it can shape **what an outer day costs** (section 4, 8).

Sources: Jensen, Radu and Sharp 2019; Miscali; M. Hailwood, "Daylight hours and work hours in early modern England", *Past &
Present* blog (2020, first 2016).

## 4. What surprised me, and what it suggests

1. **Own time came in whole days, not hours.** Every servants' law I found gives the master the working day whole, and leaves the
   servant Sundays, holy days and the days at the term: about 82 days a year in Lutheran Sweden, 22%. So the levy in hours is
   mostly the calendar's work, the same for every position. *Suggests:* compute each row as the culture's **calendar of rest days**
   (one table of holy days by season, a norm of the culture, which the Reformation and later reforms cut) times the position's share
   of a working day, rather than a free number per cell. The free week at the term is a **whole game day**, a dated grant written into
   the servant's contract, best on or near the moving round (autumn day 11), which falls where the Swedish and Danish term days fell.
2. **Positions differed in fate, wages and plots more than in hours.** The sources show no gap in hours between an heir, a younger son
   and a farmhand; they show that a younger son and a daughter were sent into service elsewhere (Norway's law of 1754: one son and
   one daughter at home), and that service paid wages, clothes and sometimes a sown patch. *Suggests:* the second son's historical
   road to his own goods ran through **service in another household**, and the potter of section 3.3 is the exception. The table can
   keep small differences (another son's winter, a daughter's evening spinning); the moving round, where a household with more adults
   than its land needs sends one into service, may matter more to sovereignty than the hours.
3. **A plot of one's own, granted as pay.** The Danish servant's lønnesæd, worked in his free weeks; the elder's right to sow flax or
   keep a cow; the crofter's croft. Own hours in the sources are usually **own hours on an own plot, granted by a contract**. *Suggests:*
   a term `plot` on a servant's or an elder's contract (a strip, a beast's fodder) is the nearest historical precedent for personal goods
   in slice 8, and needs no market.
4. **The elder owes nothing by contract.** "The majority of contracts did not specify service." *Suggests:* the elder's row is 0, and
   what the old do is their own plot (own hours) and what they give, which the design's "given back by diligence" already carries; the
   placeholder's 3 to 6 hours were a levy the sources do not show.
5. **Children worked longest in summer.** Herding was all-day work for children of seven and up, servants began at twelve, the poorest at
   six. *Suggests:* the child's summer row rises to about 10 (times its hands, which keeps the work light), and the winter row falls.
6. **Women's outer days were summer days, and their winter was textiles.** Crofters' wives owed 8 to 29 days a year, set for haymaking
   or flax, worked May to October; spinning filled women's evenings. *Suggests:* a levy's terms may name the season or the task, and a
   daughter's winter should not be read as slack.
7. **The lord named the day, but substitution was normal.** "At the first demand", the evening before or at once (Denmark 1791;
   Knutstorp's contracts), but the tenant could send "capable folk", and the larger farms kept a farmhand only for the dues. *Suggests:*
   an estate's due reads `names: 'levier'`, `notice: 0`, `substitute: true`; a household under heavy dues hires a servant, which the
   moving round can carry if the dues enter the head's estimate of hands needed.
8. **Dues were also set as land to work, not days.** Indelt hoveri gave each farm strips of the demesne to work from ploughing to harvest;
   the farm chose its days and was "then free". The village's road lots worked the same way. *Suggests:* a levy's unit may be a **task**
   (an area, a stretch of road) as well as adult-days, the levied naming its days; this is the design's custom of lots carried one step
   further, and it lets the household fit the levy around its own peaks. Lords' days also came with or without a team (spanddage,
   gangdage): a term `team: boolean` would make the oxen part of the levy.
9. **At harvest the lord's field came first.** The levy's real cost was not its days but its dates. *Suggests:* `names: 'levier'` at the
   peak is the heaviest form of power in the terms, heavier than the count of days; a measurement of the levy should report the sowing
   and harvest lost, as Study C did for routines.
10. **Commutation was common, and late.** Swedish crofters without days paid cash; Danish hoveri was commuted from the 1830s; on the
    continent dues ran from light and fixed to six days a week by region. *Suggests:* `commute` is a term that varies by levier and
    period, as the design has it; an outlying tenant's due commutable, a demesne village's not.
11. **Breach went to inspection and court, not memory.** Road inspections (vägsyn), punishments for lateness and absence (Denmark 1791),
    the meeting's fines, the courts (won by Crown tenants, lost by noble tenants), and eviction. *Suggests:* a village routine's
    `onBreach: 'fine'` at an **inspection at the window's end**, the levied having named its days before it; an estate's breach goes to
    `penalty`, and a written contract (Lindholmen's of 1688) is what a court reads.
12. **The day's length differed 7 to 11 hours by season.** *Suggests:* an outer day could cost the household hours by season (about 6,
    8, 8 and 10 hours of a game day for winter, spring, autumn and summer, after the Danish 7 : 9 : 11) instead of a flat 8.

## 5. Confidence and gaps

- **Not read at the source.** The Swedish servants' acts themselves (hours, Sundays, evening work, a servant's own work, the number of
  children a farmer might keep at home); Uppenberg's dissertation (2018) and chapter on the acts (2023); Harnesk's *Legofolk* (1990);
  Malmstedt's *Helgdagsreduktionen* (1994); Rahikainen (2020) and Olsson (2006) beyond their abstracts; Østhus on servant law in
  Denmark-Norway (2023). The open-access volume holding Østhus's and Uppenberg's chapters would not download.
- **Guesses.** Every count of village days; the outlying frälse tenant's days in Sweden; the chores on rest days (a quarter of a
  day); the differences between heir, another son, daughter and kin; the lodger's harvest days; the child's hours. The servant's and
  heir's rows rest on arithmetic over a sourced calendar and on the sourced rule that the master held the working day; their hours
  per real working day are not themselves sourced.
- **Period.** The best evidence is from the 18th and 19th centuries (Scania, Denmark, crofters). Before 1600 I have the
  Danish weekday tenant, the Swedish holy-day count about 1500, Bohemia's unlimited robot before 1680, and England about 1300; for
  1300 to 1500 in Sweden itself, nothing on numbers.
- **Disagreements.** The late medieval holy days: Denmark about 50, Sweden about 30 on weekdays (if 80 days less 50 Sundays), England
  from a handful to 30. Working year: 184 days for Danish day labourers, 150 for Malmö builders, 250 often assumed. These are wage
  labourers' years, not a household member's.
- **Finland.** Swedish law applied; I found only the holy days of Savo and Rahikainen's abstract, nothing on Finnish servants' or
  crofters' days in the period.
- **Harvest on holy days.** No source for a general exemption; the 1686 half-days allowed work after the service.
