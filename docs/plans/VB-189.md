**The history behind answers 8 and 12** (researched in the session; the marks are the session's and are kept). In early medieval
Scandinavia and England nobody called a market; it rode on a gathering that met anyway on a customary date: the assembly (the Disting
at Uppsala [secondary]); Sunday at church and saints' days in England, markets "held by custom" (Letters, *Gazetteer of Markets and
Fairs in England and Wales to 1516* [checked]); fishing seasons (the Scania herring market [secondary]); landing places whose peace
belonged to the gathering (Sindbæk 2009 [checked]). Lords and kings first guarded and tolled existing markets and granted them only
after about 1200. Markets are periodic because one place has too little demand (Skinner 1964 [summary]), the day fixed by custom and
staggered against neighbours so that pedlars can make a circuit [memory]. **Not verified:** Snorri's text, the Swedish *landsköp*
ban, and Swedish fairs on church dedication days; answer 12's "as a parish's feast day" rests on the English evidence only.

## 2. What the two mocks found

Both are throwaway research code under `docs/cities/mocks/`; `docs/cities/mock-town-emergence.md` has every finding with the run that
gives it, the method, what was invented and what a toy cannot show. The numbers below are the mocks', not the game's.

**The first mock** (`craft.js`, `market.js`; findings 1 to 14).

- The crafts pay well by the hour and are starved of buyers: a farm gives a smith 4.7 rations a year, a potter 3.0, a weaver 16; a
  household of six needs 216 (finding 1).
- The craft's share of a household's food rises smoothly with the farms in reach; there is no jump. A smith is half fed by its craft at
  50 farms and holds no field at 160 if cautious, 50 if bold (findings 2, 3). The partial town is the normal state.
- Most dealing is a walk to a maker's house on an ordinary day (72% to 98%), not a date (finding 10).
- A district of 160 farms carries about 8 households with no field and 12 half fed by craft; on a full island the farms spare food for
  14 households in an average year and under one in a bad year (findings 6, 8).
- Dates did not lock in, and a head start for a poor site vanished (finding 14).

**The second mock** (`move.js`; findings 15 to 25).

- **Makers spread; they do not gather** (15). The fullest village holds 2.3 to 3.9 of an island's 17 to 21 maker households on the real
  maps, 8 to 13 of 82 to 89 at four times the households: 10% to 19%, and 10% to 22% over 24 variants.
- **A place begun gathered comes apart** (16): 4.6 maker households at year 10, 1.4 at year 60; it leads at year 60 in none of 30 runs.
- **Reach sets where makers are, smoothly; the gathering's host holds 1.3 to 1.8 times as many** (17). Three or more makers: 3% of
  villages with under 20 households within 3 hours' walk, 46% at 40 to 80, 73% at 160 to 320.
- **Which place leads wanders** at the real sizes (four to six leaders in 10 runs); with 516 households the most central host leads in
  10 of 10, by geography (18).
- **Places an island has:** one to three villages with three makers at the late reference's 1,000 people, none with eight (19).
- **A place stops at its makers' shared custom, not at food,** unless the island is full: 19 to 36 people at the fullest place at the
  real sizes, 50 to 76 at four times; on a full island a quarter of wants go unmet for want of grain (20).
- **63% to 85% of a place's makers still hold a field;** a maker with none is under 0.85 of its need in 31% of years (21).
- **A bad year on a full island:** a maker's craft brings 64 rations, not 99; makers with no field go hungry in 58% of cases, not
  42%; the island's green dealing days fall from 45 to 30 (22).
- **Gatherings carry 23% to 40% of the makers' dealing,** most of it at the yearly gathering; rites on each village's own days raise
  it to 50%, ale evenings counted as gatherings to 59% to 73% (23).
- **The walk to ore or clay did not decide where makers live** (24); **and moving itself matters little** beyond letting a third more
  would-be makers find a place on a small island (25).
- **The label:** dealing days on the green run from 0 to 7 with no gap, capped by the calendar; work that is not farming moves from
  239 to 257 to 287 per 1,000 between a village with no maker and the fullest place. The only two-group measure is the households of
  other villages that dealt on a green in a year: under 3 at 87% of village-years, 24 and more at the hosts.

**What it means for the slice.** Three things the draft took for granted are not so: that makers would gather where buyers come; that
a market's frequency would grow; and that a placed town would hold. What the mechanics give before the ship is a countryside of
part-farming craftsmen and a yearly market at each host. That is close to the history in answer 8, and to the cities review's "few,
small, late". It is not a town.

## 5. The design

`design.md` holds it now (section 8 lists where); this section keeps the reasoning and the parts too detailed for the snapshot.

### 5.1 The label (answers 1, 11)

A place has no tier. It has measures, read at the year's end over the last 3 years, and a word the page and the reports put on them:

- **o, outsiders:** households of other villages that dealt on its green, a year. This is what the label reads.
- **d, dealing days on the green:** gathering days on which at least 3 households dealt there. Reported beside it.
- **w, work that is not farming:** of the hours its people worked, the share on crafts, carrying to deal and service (section 5.8).

**Market place:** `o` at least 12 at 3 year ends running; dropped under 6 at 3 year ends running. The two numbers are read off the
second mock's histogram, where `o` falls into two groups (under 3 at 87% of village-years, 24 and more at the hosts), and wait for
the first real run. In the mock a market place is the host of a gathering, and nothing else is. **Town:** no cut point. Neither mock
produced a place the word fits, and the measures that would carry it vary smoothly and little; the reports print `o`, `d` and `w`
for every place. A household's own label is the ladder the design has (side craft, craft household, specialist), read from the
craft's share of its food over 3 years.

Like a pawn's occupation (VB-365), the label is a derived view: nothing in the simulation reads it, and a lint line holds that.

### 5.2 Dealing on the green, and carrying goods there on an estimate (answers 7, 8)

Every occasion on a green is a place to deal: the village's meeting, the season's rite, the yearly gathering. Those present with
goods deal by the fair's rules (VB-302: proposals in seeded order, partners first, then 4 others; need decides; customary worth books).
Nothing is called and nothing is scheduled for dealing: the occasion is in the calendar for its own reason.

- **Who is there.** Whoever came to the occasion for its own sake (VB-522's piety, the meeting's holders, VB-295's goers). And a
  household of another village may send one goer only to deal, if its estimate of what it will deal on that green on that kind of day
  is worth the walk there and back at what an hour is worth in that season.
- **The estimate is the household's own** (principle 14): for each green it has been to, and each kind of day (a village's own day, a
  yearly gathering), the customary worth it dealt there, blended 0.7 old to 0.3 new at each visit. A green never visited is judged by
  what the household has heard (stories, VB-523; until then, half of what its own village's green gives it).
- **Carrying.** On its planning day before an occasion the head packs what it means to deal (what it holds over its targets, VB-698)
  as a load, if its estimate of that green covers the carrying. At its own green it carries a few steps, so it nearly always does.
- **The record.** One line for each green's day (folded into yearly counts, rule 8): the households that dealt, those of other
  villages, the worth by good. "A market" is what the chronicle calls a day whose line is long; the label reads the yearly counts.

An ale evening is at a farm, not on a green, and is not such an occasion (the plan so far's choice 3 (c), which Kim left).

### 5.3 The walk to a named household (answer 7)

Between gatherings a pawn deals at someone's door. A head that lacks a good it cannot make, and knows a household that has it, plans a
walk there on its planning day: a journey of VB-287, within a day there and back, carrying what it means to give. At the door it
offers by VB-690 and the two swap by VB-302's command, or the other says no and the refusal is remembered. What a household knows is
bounded: for each good, up to 4 households it has dealt with or seen dealing (rule 2). Nothing is counted for the label; the walk is
reported (walks a household a year, the share of worth dealt at doors: 60% to 77% in the mock).

### 5.4 Rites on a village's own days (answer 12)

VB-522 holds the season's rite on the same three days in every village (spring day 1, summer day 6, winter day 7), attended by the
village's own adults. Under answer 12 each village keeps its three rites on days of its own, and the adults of villages within the
rite's reach (2 walking hours) may come, by their piety, less as the walk grows.

- **The days.** Drawn once, at the village's founding or the scenario's start: each within `RITE.spread` 5 days after the season's
  day (spring 1 to 6, summer 6 to 11, winter 7 to 12; winter days 1 to 3 are the gathering's), from a seeded stream. Nothing staggers
  a village's days against its neighbours' but chance: staggering them by rule would set the outcome (principle 8), and with a
  median of 4 villages within reach and 6 days to fall on, most neighbours' rites fall on a free day anyway. VB-775 tries 3, 5 and 8.
- **As a custom: a rule of the culture, not a dimension of `Culture.DIMS`** (decided by principle 5; a close call, surfaced).
  `design.md` has two kinds: the voted practices, on which every pawn holds an attitude that drifts and the meeting votes, and the
  lighter rules of a culture (hunting, the sharing of common hay), which the whole culture holds and which become voted practices
  "once villages have reason to differ". The rite's days are the second kind: `own` (the one every culture starts with) or `common`
  (the same days everywhere, VB-522 as built). The reasons: nothing yet gives a pawn a stake in one option over the other, so a
  voted dimension would carry attitudes, drift and votes that move nothing; the days themselves are each village's own data, so
  villages already differ under it; `common` keeps VB-522's behaviour as the before of a before-and-after; and Kim's later review of
  customs (principle 5) finds it with the others. When an established Church takes the rite over (slice 8), its doctrine may claim
  the key.
- **What it gives.** In the mock the greens' share of the makers' dealing rises from 36% to 50%. In the game it first gives VB-522's
  purpose more reach (ties and courting across villages), which VB-775 measures before any dealing exists.
- **The history's mark.** English markets on Sundays and saints' days are checked; a Swedish parallel (fairs on church dedication
  days) is not verified.

### 5.5 Fields by all the household's food, on the head's estimate (answer 5)

- **What a head remembers.** For each source of food its household has (its fields; its animals; fish; hunting and gathering; what
  its craft and its days of work brought in; what it was given), the rations of each of the last 8 years. From that, two readings:
  its poor year (the second lowest) and its mean.
- **How far it trusts a source** is the head's own: its caution `c` (0 bold to 1 cautious) gives `c x poor + (1 - c) x mean`. Caution
  is the head's bias of VB-254 read on this scale, with VB-476's lean when that exists; no new trait.
- **Fields.** `fieldsWanted` becomes: enough fields for `FIELDS.want` of the year's rations, less what the head trusts its other
  sources to bring, at `FIELDS.expect` of the yield net of seed; as many as its hands can till; and none if the rest covers it. A
  household that lives by its cows, its nets or its loom lets fields go, slowly (at most one a year), as its poor years stop
  frightening it.

This is built before any craft exists: fish, milk and meat are sources today. It changes behaviour today for every household with a
second source of food, so VB-768 reports grain, hungry household-days and growth before and after at founders and populous, on named
seeds, for households and not only the mean; principle 2 judges it.

### 5.6 Plots, and a landless maker's plot (answers 2, 6, 9, 13)

A plot is what `sim/expansion` already lays: a house a village grows. There is no burgage kind, no street and no rule about fields; a
plot household holds what land it can get (VB-697's ask, a field bought or let when slice 5 has land rights).

Who may ask for a plot elsewhere for custom's sake is the landless with a craft: a younger son with a skill (VB-360), a servant. A
maker with a farm stays on it. Such an adult holds, among the rungs of its wish (VB-680, VB-692), "a plot at V", valued by its own
estimate of its custom there: the households it has dealt with or seen dealing, by village, shared with the makers of its craft it
knows of there; against its kin where it lives, the walk to its material from the nearest deposit it knows, and what fields the
answer leaves it. It asks through the offers of the reworked round: an offer at any time of the year, the round its due date, one
village a year; the move needs its yes and the receiving head's or village's. The mock says this spreads makers and gathers none,
and matters most on a small island, where it lets a third more would-be makers find a place; it is kept small for that reason
(VB-772).

### 5.7 Crowding and the sink (answer 3)

Each household's crowding is the people living in houses within 2 hex steps of its house, its own included, `n`; its members' hazard
is multiplied by `1 + CROWD.per x max(0, n - CROWD.free)`, at most `CROWD.max` (0.035, 36, 2.5). Measured on populous small (the
draft's probe): people within 2 steps of a house, median 14 to 17, 99th percentile 31 to 41, most 52 (seed 7, year 40: factor 1.56).
So almost no village house pays it, the densest of a large old village does, and plots grown close together would: 1.1 at four plot
households together, 1.5 to 1.63 at six and more (the second mock's arithmetic, not a measurement). No rule reads whether a place is
a town.

For each place the reports give `M / (d - b)` and `S / c` by decade, with which binds. In the mock neither binds a place before its
makers' shared custom does, but on a full island: there the farms' grain binds first (finding 20).

The scenario "The market town" (answer 15; VB-778) sets eight plot households close together on a populous island so that both can
be measured on a dense place. It is known to decay (finding 16) and is not evidence that a town emerges.

### 5.8 The work line, and what the 325 then reads (answers 4, 14)

Two lines are reported: **heads** (households by their relation to production: elite by VB-318's view when it exists; the rest by the
household's ladder), and **work**, in full-time equivalents of hours worked:

| Counted as | Hours |
| --- | --- |
| Farming | Fields, hay, herding and milking, fishing, hunting and gathering, and the carrying they need |
| Not farming | **All craft hours** (Kim): spinning and weaving, smelting and mending, pots, tar, brewing, a mill's or a kiln's work, whoever they are for; carrying goods to deal and the dealing; service and days worked for another household's craft |
| Household work, **reported apart** and outside the three figures | Cooking, water, firewood, grinding at the quern, building and upkeep (answer 14; the first mock read 403 with grinding and building in, 239 without) |
| Elite | The hours of the members of an elite household (VB-318), by what they do |

**What the middle figure reads.** When slice 6's goods exist, every farm household spends about 242 hours a year on dress, iron and
pots beside 769 on its food: 239 per 1,000. So the line does not count specialists. It reads **how much of the island's working
time is not spent getting food**, and 325 says "a third". The second mock's makers lift it to 263 to 275. To read 325 the island
needs about 128 more such hours for each farm household a year, half as much again as the 242: for instance 1.4 more lengths of
cloth a household (made for a feast, a dowry, a patron, an exchange), or the elite's building and service, or carrying by sea. And
the line **falls as work specialises**, since a master makes in 71 hours the cloth a novice makes in 153. So beside the line the
report gives the share of non-farm hours whose product left the household, which is the one that rises with specialisation.

**Honestly: the target is not expected to be met before the ship.** The middle figure reads 263 to 275 in the mock against 325, and
the 75 needs slice 5's elite. This plan does not change the target, re-record anything or write an allowance: only Kim changes a
target. The report prints the measure, the target and the residual each night and never fails. The band's width the draft proposed
(550 to 700 / 250 to 400 / 50 to 100) is printed as proposed and is not applied, since a band from 250 would make the residual
vanish. **What Kim might do, in a line:** keep 600 / 325 / 75 as the end game's, after the ship and the elite, and name what the
island should read before the ship (about 700 / 275 / 25 by the mock) as a second, earlier band; or keep one target and accept the
residual until the ship. It is left as a `decision` note.

Today the line reads near 1,000 / 0 / 0: no cloth, iron or pots are made yet (fish drying and tar are a few points).
