town and the roads). It is designed **without silver, the Crown or the Church**: on barter, obligations and followers. Most of what it
stands on is planned and not built.

So, plainly:

- **Designed firmly now**, because what it rests on is built or needs no market: the reading of 600 / 325 / 75 and its probe; the urban
  sink's report (`M / (d - b)` and `S / c`); crowding as a contribution to `death.hazard`; market potential and regions as derived views
  on the walking-hours table; the layout of burgage plots on the hex grid; the tiers and their hysteresis as a rule over quantities that
  will exist.
- **Designed as a shape, with numbers to confirm when their parts land:** the market day (it is the fair's barter at a smaller venue, so
  its rules are VB-302's); how town households get their food (it needs the fair, days owed and craft goods); the pull (it is the offers
  model's, VB-682, applied to town heads).
- **Left as a skeleton:** the city tier; a landed household's town house; trade rights of a town against its countryside (the
  *landsköp* question); anything paid in coin or sold to ships; disease in towns. Each is named where it would go, and nothing is
  designed for it here.

Two consequences shape the slice. First, before the ship the town has **no outside food**: it is fed only from what the island's farms
choose to part with, so it is the residual claimant (the balance review's finding: short at a yield 2% lower). Towns will be small and
few until the ship, which is history's answer too (Sweden about 4% urban around 1500). Second, a town can be placed by a scenario
before any fair grows one ("the world a scenario starts with is placed, not carried"), so the town's own rules can be built and measured
on a placed market place long before a grown one appears (section 7).

## 1. History, briefly, and where it leads

- **Few, small, late.** Medieval Swedish towns rarely passed 300 to 500 people; Visby and Stockholm were the exceptions. About 4% of
  Swedes lived in towns around 1500, and urbanisation roughly doubled in the agrarian crisis after the Black Death (cities review,
  section 2). The design's town of about 50 among 1,000 is of that order; 325 in "towns and specialised work" is not, and is reached only
  by counting rural specialists and hours (section 3.8).
- **Founded or privileged, but on a market that was there.** Sigtuna was a royal foundation; *köpingar* were market places, many held by
  custom before any grant; the Crown later tried to keep trade in towns by law (Magnus Eriksson's town law, about 1350; the *landsköp*
  bans, repeated for centuries, so broken). Without the Crown, this slice has only the "grown" road: a gathering's fair that comes to be
  held more often, until people live by it. The "founded" road is the Crown's, with the ship.
- **Agrarian towns.** Nordic burghers kept cattle on the town's meadow and held town fields (*stadsjord*); a town's plots had yards and
  kitchen gardens. A town was not wholly fed by the market. (From general knowledge of Swedish town history; no source was checked here.)
- **Sinks of people.** Stockholm in the 1750s and 1760s died at about 52 per 1,000; London grew only by migration (cities review). A small
  town's surplus of deaths was smaller; its servants, who did not marry while in service, did more for the balance of births than its
  deaths did (cities review, section 4).
- **The market's reach is half a day.** A market day's catchment was a walk there, a morning's dealing and a walk home (Bracton's six and
  two-thirds miles; markets review 3.1); a fair's is a day's walk.

**Where I am unsure.** The town fields and cattle point is from memory; the scale of a market day in game time (a game day is about a
week, so "a weekly market" is a market every game day) is my reading of the calendar; and whether servants in a town count as peasants
(the balance review's row) is ambiguous (section 3.8).

## 2. Decided by the principles (each with its reason)

1. **A town grows out of a gathering by one quantity, how often its market is held,** and the tier reads that and the households who
   live by it (`design.md`, From gathering to town, Proposed; markets review 5.7). Reason: principle 8 (mechanisms, not prescriptions): the
   count of trading households is the result; frequency is what lets them live.
2. **A market date is added or dropped by a pawn:** the host village's meeting decides next year's dates at its autumn meeting, and the
   alderman carries it out. He proposes from what he saw: his estimate of how full last year's dates were (principles 14 and 16). No
   date appears because a threshold of the simulation was crossed.
3. **No town is placed by a rule, and none is given a size** (principle 13, "An elite is what the rules leave over"; target-v2 4.6: "about
   two cities a map should emerge from geography, not a quota").
4. **Food reaches a town only by being carried:** a farm household's goer brings a load to the market day and carries home what it
   took; a town household carries home what it got, a few steps. Rent, a keep or days owed in grain are carried by the one who owes
   them to the creditor's store (the hard rule; principle 9: the cost of carrying is what makes a route worth having).
5. **Place decides who feeds the town:** a household goes to a market day only when what it expects to gain covers the hours of the
   walk there and back at the worth of a day's work, by its head's estimate from its last visits (principle 14). Near farms sell to the
   town and far ones rarely do, so rings of supply form round a town by distance, with no ring drawn (von Thünen's result from the
   carrying cost; principle 9).
6. **A town goes short first.** Need decides acceptance (target-v2 5.1): a farm whose stores will not carry it to the next harvest with
   its reserve values food at up to 3 times its worth and does not sell; food beyond two years' need it values at 0.5 and sells readily
   (`design.md`, The fair, Need). So the cities review's two rules (sell only above the reserve; in a dearth the town goes short) follow
   from the fair's need curve, and no rule of the town's is added. Reason: principles 1 and 8.
7. **The town pulls through offers, never a score of the town:** a town head short of hands offers service; a landless adult who wants
   a plot asks for one; whoever moves answers yes (principle 16; Kim's answers of 16 November to VB-682: a move needs the pawn's yes and
   the receiving head's). There is no "town attractiveness".
8. **The urban penalty is a contribution to `death.hazard`, from crowding,** not a flag on town people (principles 3 and 8: a named
   tunable with a physical meaning; target-v2 principle 11, rules combine contributions). It stands in for sickness until disease exists
   (slice 8, now last); when disease comes, crowding feeds its contact rate and this contribution shrinks by what disease then takes.
9. **Settlement tier and region are derived views with hysteresis, never stored as truth** (target-v2 principle 7). Their caches have
   one writer.
10. **Customs, not rules, for what towns differ in:** who may marry in town, whether lodgers are taken (principle 5). Defaults lean to the
    history that keeps the sink working.

## 3. The design

### 3.1 From gathering to market place to town

**The calendar.** A game day stands for about a week, a season of 12 days for about three months. So, in game time: a yearly fair is
the gathering (winter days 1 to 3); "twice a year" adds a date before sowing; "monthly" is a date every 4 days (12 a year); "weekly" is a
date every game day (48 a year).

**Market dates.** The host of a gathering keeps a list of market dates, one entry a date, in the due-queue (rule 4). Slice 4 builds the
yearly gathering only; slice 9 lets the list grow. A market date is a small occasion at the host village's green (later its square):

- **Who comes:** households within `MARKET.reach`, 3 walking hours one way by the table of walking hours (VB-286), a half day's round trip
  with the dealing; one goer a household, its holder or spokesman, with up to one load (30 rations on the back, 90 by cart: The fair).
  A household goes on its planning day's estimate (Decision 5): expected gain, from what it got at its last visits, against the hours of
  walking at the worth of a day's work (3 rations for 8 hours). Town households live at the site and walk no hours, so they go to every
  date they have something to deal.
- **What happens:** the fair's barter, unchanged (VB-302): proposals in seeded order, partners first, then 4 others; need decides,
  customary worth books, the residual is goodwill. Hours 8:00 to 12:00 of the date.
- **What is counted:** households that dealt at each date, and the customary worth that changed hands; a market day's report in the log,
  as the fair's.

**How the list grows (Decision 2).** At the host's autumn meeting (day 10) its alderman proposes next year's dates, and the meeting
decides them as it decides its routines:

- a season whose dates last year drew on average at least `MARKET.full` dealing households (8) gains dates: from 1 to 2, then to 3, then
  monthly (3 a season, 12 a year), then every second day (6 a season), then every day (12 a season);
- a season whose dates drew fewer than `MARKET.thin` (3) loses a step;
- with no alderman (slice 5 not built), the host village's holder of most standing proposes (a stand-in, removed with VB-314).

The rule's two thresholds are its hysteresis: a list that has grown does not shrink on one thin year, since it shrinks only below 3.
Rivals: two hosts within each other's reach divide the households between them, and the one with the fuller dates grows; the other's
dates thin and drop. Which survives is what market potential explains (3.2); it chooses nothing.

**The tiers.** Read yearly, at the year's end, with hysteresis by year ends (as class is, target-v2 4.6):

| Tier | Enters when, for 3 year ends running | Leaves when, for 3 year ends running | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Village | (every settlement) | | As now |
| Market place (*köping*) | At least 12 market dates a year, and at least 3 households living on plots by craft or trade | Fewer than 8 dates, or fewer than 2 such households | The culture's settlement kind `city` (attitudes move 2.2 times as fast, less anchored, weigh 3 times at the reckoning: built in `src/culture.js`); booths on the green for the gathering (The gathering, Lodging) |
| Town (*stad*) | At least 48 market dates (one every game day), and at least 10 households living on plots by craft or trade | Fewer than 32 dates, or fewer than 7 such households | Its own meeting for the plots' households (a town meeting, its own alderman); the custom "Marriage in town" (3.5) |
| City | Skeleton: left to the ship (the Crown's charter, a harbour, coin). Not entered in slice 9 | | |

"Living by craft or trade" is the ladder's third step (`design.md`, Goods of place, The ladder): a household on a burgage plot (3.3),
which holds no field, and which got most of its food over the last 3 years at market dates, from service, from rent or days owed, or
from its own fish, not from fields. A household on a plot that also farms village fields is a craft household (the ladder's second step)
and does not count. Until slice 6's crafts exist, such households are fishers, tar burners, brewers and labourers.

The three numbers per tier (dates, households, years) are named tunables (`TIER.*`). The 3 and the 10 are the cities review's and Kim's
earlier proposal ("3 households live by trade or craft"; "a market place of 10 with a church counts as one" without the Crown); there
is no church before the ship, so the church is dropped from the town's test.

### 3.2 Market potential and regions

**Potential** (Harris 1954), computed on the table of walking hours, not on tiles: for each village `v`,
`P(v) = sum over villages u within reach of people(u) x exp(-hours(u, v) / MARKET.decay)`, with `MARKET.decay` 3 hours (a half day's walk:
a village 3 hours off counts at 37%, one a day's walk off at 2%). Refreshed with the hours table, a village a step on autumn day 9.
Only villages are candidates: a market grows at a host's green, so potential need not be known for every tile (the cities review's
tile map stays a probe). By water, potential waits for boats (slice 10).

What potential is for: it explains which hosts win (it is reported beside each host's dates), it is shown on the map, and it is what a
village's meeting reads when it weighs offering to host a gathering (a term of its own wish to host; VB-294 has the choice). It sets no
tier and places nothing (Decision 3).

**Regions** (the district is the gathering's villages: `design.md`, The gathering). A village's **market** is the market place or town
whose dates its households dealt at most over the last 3 years; with none, the one it reaches in the fewest hours, if within a day's
walk. A **region** is a market place or town and the villages whose market it is. The 3-year count is the hysteresis: a village changes
region only when another centre has drawn more of its dealings over three years. (Target-v2 4.6 has "the centre it reaches most
cheaply"; `design.md` has "attendance is what is read". This draft takes attendance with reach as the fallback: Kim's choice 7.)

### 3.3 Burgage plots and their layout

A burgage plot (*tomt*) is a house tile and one yard tile behind it, with no field. It is laid out by the village whose market place it
is, as village work, like the green (`sim/green`) and the village's plots (`sim/expansion`, `village.plots`, kind `burgage`).

**When.** A market place's meeting lays out a row of plots when a landless adult has asked for one and none is free (VB-697's ask,
answered by the alderman by the `claims` and `newLand` customs), at most one row a year. The first row is laid in the year the place first
holds 12 dates.

**Where, on the hex grid.**

- **The square.** The green becomes the market square (*torget*): its tiles and the ring of open tiles round it (built:
  `rimFull`, `sim/hex`) are kept open, and the square grows to 7 tiles (a hex and its six neighbours) round the well when the place first
  becomes a market place, taking only tiles that are open or the village's own.
- **The street.** A row runs out from the square along the approach most walked (the worn path with the most crossings, VB-094): the
  street is one tile wide and kept open; plots face it from both sides, frontage one tile, the house on the street and the yard behind,
  so a pair of plots across the street is 5 tiles deep (yard, house, street, house, yard). On pointy-top hexes the street follows one of
  the three hex axes nearest the path's bearing. A row is up to 6 plots a side; a second row starts at the square on the next most walked
  approach.
- **What it may take.** Open land, the village's own land, and a field only with its holder's yes (an offer; a field bought or let is
  slice 5's land rights). A plot never cuts off a way (`cutsOff`, built).

**Why a laid street, when villages have none** (Decided, 12 Nov: "a village has no laid street, and its lanes wear in where its people
walk"). A town is the exception, with a motive: medieval Nordic towns were laid out in long narrow plots along a street (Sigtuna's Stora
gatan), because the ground at a market was scarce and valued by its frontage, and a plot was granted, not grown. This is an exception to
principle 15 ("like is shown alike") and to a Decided layout, so it is Kim's choice 2.

**Sizes.** The design's town of 35 to 40 tiles (`design.md`, Sizing at 1,000 pawns): a square of 7, two rows of plots, about 11 houses,
22 tiles of plots and 8 of street, is 37. A town of 10 to 12 plot households and their servants is 50 to 70 people.

**A plot's own food.** The yard is a kitchen garden (*kålgård*): until the crops have a garden crop it is a yard, and the household's food
comes from elsewhere. A plot household may keep one cow on the market place's common meadow, under the grazing rights of VB-141, and
hold no field. Town fields (*stadsjord*, the burghers' own fields) are left out of this slice: they would make town households half
farmers, which blurs the 325, and the town's food is the residual claimant either way (Kim's choice 3).

### 3.4 How town households get food

No coin and no price: town households barter, as everyone does at the fair. What they give:

| What a town household gives | For what | What it needs to exist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Its own goods of place: dried fish, tar, ale | Grain, butter, salt meat | Built (fish, tar); ale with the fair |
| Craft goods and mending: tools, iron, cloth, pots | Food and raw goods | Slice 6 (VB-121, VB-362, VB-364, VB-366) |
| Days of work: at a farm's peaks, carted loads, building | Grain, paid at the day's end or as days owed | Slice 5 (hired hands VB-319, days owed VB-462) |
| Credit with its partners | Food now, booked as goodwill and repaid in goods later | The goodwill balance (built) and allowance (built); partners from the fair (VB-302) |
| Keeping goods taken in earlier deals | Food | The fair's keeping goods (VB-302) |

And what it does not buy:

- **Servants eat at the master's table** (service, built); a town servant buys nothing.
- **A landed household's rent and days owed** are carried in by those who owe them; if it keeps a town house, its rent feeds it there.
  Skeleton: a landed household's town house waits for class (VB-318) and the elite's roads; nothing is designed for it here.
- **A follower is fed by his lord** only by the bonds of VB-708 (help asked of the lord first after kin), not by a rule of the town.

**What a town household plans.** Its head's plan (slice 3's standing plan) gains the market as an occasion: on each date it goes with
what it has made beyond its own need (the target of VB-698 on each stored good is what sets that), and it aims its craft at what it
lacks. Its food need curve is the farm's (The fair, Need): a town household with stores for less than the season ahead values food
highly and offers more for it, as a hungry farm does.

**In a dearth** the farms' need curves rise and they sell nothing below their reserve (Decision 6). The town goes short: its people go
hungry, ask kin for help as anyone does (the gift, built; patrons, VB-464), die more by hunger's term in the hazard (built), and its
servants' masters feed fewer at the next round. Nothing protects the town; nothing protects the farms from a town that offers well.

**Before the ship, how large can a town be?** The cities review's toy town, fed only from grain above two years' need (about what
rots), stayed at 18 to 23 people on the tiny map. On populous small the surplus of households above their need is 120 to 260 person-years
a year and 30 to 100 rot (review 3.2); a town bartering for a part of it might hold 30 to 100 people on a small map, more on a medium.
That is an estimate, not a measurement; the wrap-up measures it as `S / c`.

### 3.5 Servants and lodgers from the country

**Service in town** is service as built and planned: by the year, from round to round; the servant's home is the master's and no home to
bring a spouse to (VB-634); offered and taken (VB-694). A town head offers service when its household's hands fall short of its work:
for a craft household, when the hours of its craft and its house over the last year exceed its members' working hours by a servant's
year; for any household, by VB-694's rule of hands a field, where the "field" of a town household is its craft's hours (`SERVICE.hoursPerHand`).
A town's service offers reach as far as the round's (a day's walk, 12 hours). Who answers: unmarried landless adults, by VB-694's weights
and by their own estimate of the place (3.7).

**Lodgers** (*inhyses*). A town household with room may offer a lodging: a bed in its house for a landless adult who is not its servant,
on the basis `lodging`, a row of VB-690's table: the lodger gives days of work, `LODGING.days` (4) a season, to the household. A lodger
eats what it gets for itself: days of work at the farms' peaks and on market dates. Lodgers are the town's poor and its day labourers;
they are the people who came to town with no place, the reason history's towns grew in crises. A lodging is no home to marry into by
default (Kim's choice 5).

**Marriage in town.** A couple needs a place under the residence custom (the gate, `design.md`, Marriage, nursing and growth); in town a
plot is a holding, so a plot household's heir and a landless adult who has been given a plot may marry onto it. A new custom, "Marriage
in town" (principle 5): **with a plot** (default) or **with a plot or a lodging**. Under the default, servants and lodgers wait; that wait,
more than the town's deaths, is what makes a town a sink (cities review, section 4). Malleability 0.4.

### 3.6 The urban hazard and crowding; the sink

**Crowding** (Decision 8). A new contribution to `death.hazard`: each household's crowding is the number of people living in houses
within 2 hex steps of its house, its own included, `n`; the hazard of its members is multiplied by
`1 + CROWD.per x max(0, n - CROWD.free)`, at most `CROWD.max`.

Measured on populous small (16 November, this plan's probe): the people within 2 steps of a house are, at seed 7 year 20, 14 at the median,
26 at the 90th percentile, 35 at the 99th and 39 at the most (110 houses); at seed 42 year 40, 16, 25, 31 and 32 (160 houses); at seed 7
year 40, 17, 31, 41 and 52 (143 houses): the largest villages grow denser as they fill. A town of
two rows of plots across a street has 8 or 9 houses within 2 steps of a middle house; at 5 to 6 people a house with servants and lodgers,
45 to 55 people. Starting values: `CROWD.free` 36 (above the villages' 99th percentile), `CROWD.per` 0.035, `CROWD.max` 2.5. Then:

| People within 2 steps | Factor | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 14 to 36 | 1.00 | Almost every village house (the median 14 to 17) |
| 41 | 1.18 | The 99th percentile of village houses at seed 7, year 40 |
| 52 | 1.56 | The densest village house seen (seed 7, year 40): a large village that crowds pays as a town does |
| 50 | 1.49 | A town house of two rows (the budget's "about 1.5 times", Kim 13 Nov) |
| 60 | 1.84 | A crowded town house with lodgers (the cities review's 1.8) |

So the penalty is not a flag on town people: a village that crowds pays it, a market place of 3 spread plots does not, and a town pays
it by how it is built and how many it lodges. The 1.5 of the growth budget and the 1.8 of the cities review are two densities of one
rule. (Whether 1.5 is read as this hazard factor, age for age, or as the crude death rate, which a town of young migrants keeps lower,
is part of Kim's choice 4.)

**The sink.** A town's population settles near `M / (d - b)`, migrants a year over its excess of deaths over births, and cannot pass
`S / c`, the food that reaches it over each person's need (target-v2 6.2). Before tuning a town, the binding one is found: if food binds,
the hazard changes how many die getting there, not how many live there. Both are reported for each market place and town, by decade.
The growth budget's line for the town is 0.54 of a point of yearly growth at about 1,000; it takes its people from those the marriage gate
already holds, so it decides where people live and the valve still holds the number.

### 3.7 The pull at the moving round

There is no pull of the town. There are pawns who offer and pawns who answer, in the offers model Kim set for VB-682 (16 November): offers
are made all the year and fall due at the round; a move needs the pawn's yes and the receiving head's; a pawn pursues one rung at a time.

| Offer | Who makes it | To whom | Basis | Due |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A place in service in town | A town head short of hands (3.5) | Unmarried landless adults within a day's walk | `service` (VB-694) | The round |
| A lodging | A town household with room | The same, and its kin first | `lodging` (new row) | The round |
| A plot | Asked for by a landless adult, answered by the market place's alderman by the custom (VB-697) | | (VB-697) | The round |

**How an adult answers** (its rung: "where do I live next year"). Its weights are VB-682's, with three terms the town brings:

- **Its place at home:** hunger at home over the last year; its years waited and its place in line for land.
- **The town as it knows it:** its estimate, by what it has heard (stories, VB-523; principle 14), of whether those who went fared well:
  deaths and hunger among kin and acquaintances who live there. Until stories exist, its own kin in town (a sibling who went) is its
  estimate, and nothing else is known of the town.
- **The one who offers:** opinion of the head, as for any service offer.

So more go to town after bad harvests (history: crisis made towns), fewer when kin in town died, and none without an offer or a plot.
Children of a moving adult and a spouse follow as now. Cost: no pass over pairs; an offer goes to those within reach through the
villages' indexes of the unmarried (section 4).

### 3.8 The reading of 600 / 325 / 75

Kim's end-game target is 600 farming, 325 in towns and specialised work, 75 elite, of 1,000 (Decided; `design.md`, Strata). The proposed
reading stands (target-v2 6.3): **a band for a developed island at the end game, not a quota; strata by relation to production, not
residence.** This plan makes it countable:

- **Elite (the 75):** members of households the class view (VB-318) holds elite, landed or not (Roads to the elite: "not a class of its
  own" for office, Kim 15 Nov), with their servants counted as below.
- **Towns and specialised work (the 325):** members of households on the ladder's second or third step (a craft household, a specialist
  with a plot) wherever they live, and the members of a retinue (Decided, 12 Nov).
- **Farming (the 600):** everyone else: farm households, tenants, cottagers, and servants (Decided, 12 Nov: "servants are peasants").

Two lines are reported, because Kim also decided (12 Nov) that "farmers' slack-season hours make part of the goods, so that fewer
full-time specialists are needed than 325, is welcome":

1. **Heads,** as above.
2. **Work,** in full-time equivalents: every adult's working hours over the year split by what they went to (own and the household's
   food and fodder; craft, trade, carrying to market, service to a non-farm household; the elite's own hours by its household), summed

## 9. Design choices for Kim

1. **What makes a market place and a town?**
   (a) Market dates and households living by them, with hysteresis: a market place at 12 dates a year and 3 plot households, a town at a
   date every game day and 10 (leaving below 8 / 2 and 32 / 7, over 3 year ends); the city left to the ship.
   (b) Households only (3 and 10), as the cities review had.
   (c) Market potential alone (the top sites by Harris potential).
   (d) As (a), with a city at 30 plot households in slice 9.
   **Proposed: (a).** Frequency is what lets a household live by a market (the ladder), and the count follows from it.
2. **How are burgage plots laid out?**
   (a) A laid street from a square of 7 tiles along the most walked approach, plots a house and a yard deep, facing it from both sides: an
   exception to "no laid street" (12 Nov), with history as its motive.
   (b) Grown like village houses, round the square, with no street.
   (c) A street only once the place is a town; grown plots before.
   **Proposed: (c).** A market place of 3 to 9 plots looks like a village that has a square; a town shows that it was laid out.
3. **What food does a town household have of its own?**
   (a) None: all from deals, service and days of work.
   (b) A kitchen garden and one cow on the common meadow, no fields.
   (c) As (b), and town fields (*stadsjord*) let to burghers, as Nordic towns had.
   **Proposed: (b).** It is historical and softens the town's place as the residual claimant without making its people farmers in the count.
4. **What is the urban penalty?**
   (a) A factor of 1.5 on the hazard for anyone living in a town.
   (b) 1.8, the cities review's.
   (c) Crowding, the people within 2 steps of a house: 1 up to 36, then +0.035 each, at most 2.5 (1.5 at a town of two rows, 1.8 at one
   crowded with lodgers), standing in for disease.
   (d) Nothing until disease exists.
   **Proposed: (c),** with "about 1.5" read as the hazard age for age at a town's usual density. A mechanism with a physical meaning,
   which villages barely feel (the median village house has 14 to 17 people within 2 steps; the 99th percentile 31 to 41, measured
   on populous small, seeds 7 and 42), though the densest houses of a large old village pay as a town does.
5. **Who may marry in town, and are there lodgers?**
   (a) Only on a plot; no lodgers.
   (b) Only on a plot; lodgers (a bed for 4 days of work a season), who may not marry there.
   (c) On a plot or a lodging, lodgers too.
   (d) A custom "Marriage in town" with (b) as its default and (c) its other way.
   **Proposed: (d).** The wait of servants and lodgers is what makes a town a sink; a custom lets villages differ and drift.
6. **How are 600 / 325 / 75 counted?**
   (a) Heads, by the household's relation to production (servants farming, retinues specialists).
   (b) Work, in full-time equivalents of adults' hours, so side crafts count.
   (c) Both reported, the band read on the work line: 550 to 700 / 250 to 400 / 50 to 100, nightly from slice 9, never failing.
   (d) Both reported, with no band until the ship.
   **Proposed: (c).** Kim welcomed farmers' slack hours making part of the goods (12 Nov); only the work line can show that.
7. **Which centre does a village belong to?**
   (a) The one it reaches most cheaply (target-v2 4.6).
   (b) The one its households dealt at most over 3 years, the nearest within a day's walk as the fallback.
   (c) The one of highest potential within a day's walk.
   **Proposed: (b).** What is read is where people go; the 3-year tally is the hysteresis.
8. **When is the town built, given that the fair, slice 5's alderman and offers, and slice 6's crafts are not?**
   (a) Build the three tasks that need no market now (reports, crowding, potential); the rest after the fair (VB-302), the offers
   (VB-690, VB-694) and the alderman (VB-314), on a placed market town, without waiting for slice 6.
   (b) As (a), but wait for slice 6's crafts too, so that towns have something to live by beyond fish, tar, ale and labour.
   (c) Wait for slices 4 to 6 entirely.
   **Proposed: (a).** The town's own rules can be measured on a placed scenario early, and fishers and labourers are a historical first town.
