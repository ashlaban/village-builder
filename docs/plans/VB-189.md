# VB-189: the town (slice 9), the plan so far

16 November 2026. Phase two of an attended plan. The first draft (this file at the run's earlier commits) put eight choices to Kim; a
first mock (`docs/cities/mock-town-emergence.md`, part one) put six more; Kim answered, and asked for a second mock, which is part two
of the same document. This is the plan as it now stands: Kim's answers as the frame, what the two mocks found, what falls from the
draft, the mechanisms the game needs with a task for each, the firm parts as task sketches, the text proposed for `design.md`, and
what still cannot be planned. Nothing here is written into `design.md`, the roadmap, the backlog or `QUESTIONS.md` yet: Kim sees it first.

## 0. In brief

- **The second mock's main finding is negative.** With a market that nobody calls, dealing on the green at gatherings that happen
  anyway, walks to a maker's house, and makers free to ask for a plot where their custom is best, **makers spread out; they do not
  gather.** The fullest village holds 10% to 22% of an island's makers in every variant tried, a place begun gathered comes apart in a
  generation, and nothing locks in. An island of the real maps' size ends with 17 to 21 part-farming maker households, one to three
  villages with three of them, and one busy market: the three days of the yearly gathering at each district's host.
- **So before the ship the label "market place" has something to read, and "town" has not.** The one natural cut point either mock
  found is the green where households of other villages deal, which is the gathering's host. What would gather makers into a town
  (a shared kiln or bloomery, a landed household's table, a harbour, a granted market) is outside both mocks and mostly outside what
  is built. That is Kim's first choice below.
- **Six tasks are firm and four of them need no market** (section 7): the strata and work-line report; crowding; a head's remembered
  yield of each food source; fields sized against all the household's food; and, waiting for the fair's swap, dealing on the green
  at any gathering and the walk to a named household. Four more are sketched and wait for what they read.
- **Not plannable yet:** anything that needs the elite, a facility shared between makers, service and lodging in a town, or a number
  for dealing before the fair exists (section 9).

## 1. The frame: Kim's answers of 16 November

These stand. Each is quoted or closely paraphrased; where the plan had to read one, the reading is said.

   reads it; no threshold switches anything on.
2. **Plots are always grown,** as village houses are. No laid street.
3. **The urban penalty is crowding:** the people within 2 steps of a house; none up to 36, +0.035 each beyond, at most 2.5.
4. **600 / 325 / 75 is counted by work** in full-time equivalents, heads beside it; and **all craft hours count as work that is not

5. **Town food appears by mechanics.** A craftsman who cannot yet live by the craft keeps farming, herding, a cow, hired days. The head

   the craft's poor years, a bold one its mean (principle 14: the estimate is the head's).
6. **A plot household may hold whatever land it can get.** No rule about fields.
7. **Dealing:** a gathering day's dealing is on the village green, and everyone taking part, buyers and sellers, walks there. On other
   days a pawn may still walk to a named household to deal. A place's label counts dealing days on the green.
8. **Market days ride on gatherings.** Kim: "It feels weird to me that a single person calls a market." No pawn calls a market; pawns
   bring goods to gatherings that happen anyway (the meeting day, a rite, the seasonal gathering, the yearly gathering of VB-294),
   each by its own estimate of who will come, learned from past days; a day with much dealing is what the record calls a market; a
   granted market waits for the Crown. The first mock's host-called date and its break-even fall.
9. **Makers:** "A maker may move to its buyers, after a second mock. Note that moving may have been redesigned (or is about to; the
   dependency on a fixed 'moving round' is being reworked)." Section 10 says what this plan assumed of VB-682.

**The history behind answer 8** (researched in the session; the marks are the session's and are kept). In early medieval Scandinavia
and England nobody called a market; it rode on a gathering that met anyway on a customary date: the assembly (the Disting at Uppsala
[secondary]); Sunday at church and saints' days in England, markets "held by custom" (Letters, *Gazetteer of Markets and Fairs in
England and Wales to 1516* [checked]); fishing seasons (the Scania herring market [secondary]); landing places whose peace belonged to
the gathering (Sindbæk 2009 [checked]). Lords and kings first guarded and tolled existing markets and granted them only after about
1200. Markets are periodic because one place has too little demand (Skinner 1964 [summary]), the day fixed by custom and staggered
against neighbours so that pedlars can make a circuit [memory]. Not verified: Snorri's text, the Swedish *landsköp* ban, Swedish fairs
on church dedication days.

## 2. What the two mocks found

Both are throwaway research code under `docs/cities/mocks/`; `docs/cities/mock-town-emergence.md` has every finding with the run that
gives it, the method, what was invented and what a toy cannot show. The numbers below are the mocks', not the game's.

**The first mock** (`craft.js`, `market.js`; findings 1 to 14).

- The crafts pay well by the hour and are starved of buyers: a farm gives a smith 4.7 rations a year, a potter 3.0, a weaver 16; a
  household of six needs 216 (finding 1).
- The craft's share of a household's food rises smoothly with the farms in reach; there is no jump. A smith is half fed by its craft at
  50 farms and holds no field at 160 if cautious, 50 if bold (findings 2, 3). The partial town is the normal state.
- Most dealing is a walk to a maker's house on an ordinary day (72% to 98%), not a date (finding 10).
- A district of 160 farms carries about 8 households with no field and 12 half fed by craft; on a full island the farms spare food for
  14 households in an average year and under one in a bad year (findings 6, 8).
- Dates did not lock in, and a head start for a poor site vanished (finding 14).

**The second mock** (`move.js`; findings 15 to 25).

- **Makers spread; they do not gather** (15). The fullest village holds 2.3 to 3.9 of an island's 17 to 21 maker households on the real
  maps, 8 to 13 of 82 to 89 at four times the households: 10% to 19%, and 10% to 22% over 24 variants.
- **A place begun gathered comes apart** (16): 4.6 maker households at year 10, 1.4 at year 60; it leads at year 60 in none of 30 runs.
- **Reach sets where makers are, smoothly; the gathering's host holds 1.3 to 1.8 times as many** (17). Three or more makers: 3% of
  villages with under 20 households within 3 hours' walk, 46% at 40 to 80, 73% at 160 to 320.
- **Which place leads wanders** at the real sizes (four to six leaders in 10 runs); with 516 households the most central host leads in
  10 of 10, by geography (18).
- **Places an island has:** one to three villages with three makers at the late reference's 1,000 people, none with eight (19).
- **A place stops at its makers' shared custom, not at food,** unless the island is full: 19 to 36 people at the fullest place at the
  real sizes, 50 to 76 at four times; on a full island a quarter of wants go unmet for want of grain (20).
- **63% to 85% of a place's makers still hold a field;** a maker with none is under 0.85 of its need in 31% of years (21).
- **A bad year on a full island:** a maker's craft brings 64 rations, not 99; makers with no field go hungry in 58% of cases, not
  42%; the island's green dealing days fall from 45 to 30 (22).
- **Gatherings carry 23% to 40% of the makers' dealing,** most of it at the yearly gathering; rites on each village's own days raise
  it to 50%, ale evenings counted as gatherings to 59% to 73% (23).
- **The walk to ore or clay did not decide where makers live** (24); **and moving itself matters little** beyond letting a third more
  would-be makers find a place on a small island (25).
- **The label:** dealing days on the green run from 0 to 7 with no gap, capped by the calendar; work that is not farming moves from
  239 to 257 to 287 per 1,000 between a village with no maker and the fullest place. The only two-group measure is the households of
  other villages that dealt on a green in a year: under 3 at 87% of village-years, 24 and more at the hosts.

**What it means for the slice.** Three things the draft took for granted are not so: that makers would gather where buyers come; that
a market's frequency would grow; and that a placed town would hold. What the mechanics give before the ship is a countryside of
part-farming craftsmen and a yearly market at each host. That is close to the history in answer 8, and to the cities review's "few,
small, late". It is not a town.

## 5. The design so far

### 5.1 The label (answers 1, 7)

A place has no tier. It has measures, read at the year's end over the last 3 years, and a word the page and the reports put on them:

- **d, dealing days on the green:** gathering days on which at least 3 households dealt there.
- **o, outsiders:** households of other villages that dealt on its green, a year.
- **w, work that is not farming:** of the hours its people worked, the share on crafts, carrying to deal and service (section 5.7).

**Market place:** `o` at least 12 for 3 year ends running; it is dropped under 6 for 3 year ends. These two numbers are read off the
second mock's histogram, where `o` falls into two groups, and wait for the first real run. In the mock a market place is the host of
a gathering, and nothing else is. **Town:** no cut point is proposed. Neither mock produced a place the word fits, and the measures
that would carry it vary smoothly and little; the reports print `d`, `o` and `w` for every place, and the word waits for a place that
earns it (Kim's choice 1). A household's own label is the ladder the design has (side craft, craft household, specialist), read from
the craft's share of its food over 3 years.

Like a pawn's occupation (VB-365), the label is a derived view: nothing in the simulation reads it, and a lint line holds that.

### 5.2 Dealing on the green, and carrying goods there on an estimate (answers 7, 8)

Every occasion on a green is a place to deal: the village's meeting, the season's rite, the yearly gathering. Those present with
goods deal by the fair's rules (VB-302: proposals in seeded order, partners first, then 4 others; need decides; customary worth books).
Nothing is called and nothing is scheduled for dealing: the occasion is in the calendar for its own reason.

- **Who is there.** Whoever came to the occasion for its own sake (VB-522's piety, the meeting's holders, VB-295's goers). And a
  household of another village may send one goer only to deal, if its estimate of what it will deal on that green on that kind of day
  is worth the walk there and back at what an hour is worth in that season.
- **The estimate is the household's own** (principle 14): for each green it has been to, and each kind of day (a village's own day, a
  yearly gathering), the customary worth it dealt there, blended 0.7 old to 0.3 new at each visit. A green never visited is judged by
  what the household has heard (stories, VB-523; until then, half of what its own village's green gives it).
- **Carrying.** On its planning day before an occasion the head packs what it means to deal (what it holds over its targets, VB-698)
  as a load, if its estimate of that green covers the carrying. At its own green it carries a few steps, so it nearly always does.
- **The record.** One line for each green's day (folded into yearly counts, rule 8): the households that dealt, those of other
  villages, the worth by good. "A market" is what the chronicle calls a day whose line is long; the label reads the yearly counts.

Two things this leaves open, both Kim's (choice 3): whether an ale evening is such an occasion (it is at a farm, not on the green),
and whether rites fall on the same days everywhere (as VB-522 has them: then a household cannot go to a neighbour's rite, and in the
mock the green's share of dealing is 36%, against 50% with each village's rite on its own day).

### 5.3 The walk to a named household (answer 7)

Between gatherings a pawn deals at someone's door. A head that lacks a good it cannot make, and knows a household that has it, plans a
walk there on its planning day: a journey of VB-287, within a day there and back, carrying what it means to give. At the door it
offers by VB-690 and the two swap by VB-302's command, or the other says no and the refusal is remembered. What a household knows is
bounded: for each good, up to 4 households it has dealt with or seen dealing (rule 2). Nothing is counted for the label; the walk is
reported (walks a household a year, the share of worth dealt at doors: 60% to 77% in the mock).

### 5.4 Fields by all the household's food, on the head's estimate (answer 5)

- **What a head remembers.** For each source of food its household has (its fields; its animals; fish; what its craft and its days of
  work brought in; what it was given), the rations of each of the last 8 years. From that, two readings: its poor year (the second
  lowest) and its mean.
- **How far it trusts a source** is the head's own: its caution `c` (0 bold to 1 cautious) gives `c x poor + (1 - c) x mean`. Caution
  is the head's bias of VB-254 read on this scale, with VB-476's lean when that exists; no new trait.
- **Fields.** `fieldsWanted` becomes: enough fields for `FIELDS.want` of the year's rations, less what the head trusts its other
  sources to bring, at `FIELDS.expect` of the yield net of seed; as many as its hands can till; and none if the rest covers it. A
  household that lives by its cows, its nets or its loom puts fields down, slowly, as its poor years stop frightening it.

This is built before any craft exists: fish, milk and meat are sources today. It changes behaviour today, so it reports its numbers
(VB-768).

### 5.5 Plots, and a maker's plot (answers 2, 6, 9)

A plot is what `sim/expansion` already lays: a house a village grows. There is no burgage kind, no street and no rule about fields; a
plot household holds what land it can get (VB-697's ask, a field bought or let when slice 5 has land rights).

A landless adult with a craft (a younger son with a skill, VB-360) holds, among the rungs of its wish (VB-680, VB-692), "a plot at V",
valued by its own estimate of its custom there: the households it has dealt with or seen dealing, by village, shared with the makers
of its craft it knows of there; against its kin where it lives, the walk to its material from the nearest deposit it knows, and what
fields the alderman's answer leaves it. It asks one village a year (one rung at a time); the alderman answers by the custom (VB-697).
The mock says this spreads makers and gathers none, and matters most on a small island; it is kept small for that reason (VB-772).

### 5.6 Crowding and the sink (answer 3)

As the draft had it. Each household's crowding is the people living in houses within 2 hex steps of its house, its own included, `n`;
its members' hazard is multiplied by `1 + CROWD.per x max(0, n - CROWD.free)`, at most `CROWD.max` (0.035, 36, 2.5). Measured on
populous small (the draft's probe): people within 2 steps of a house, median 14 to 17, 99th percentile 31 to 41, most 52 (seed 7,
year 40: factor 1.56). So almost no village house pays it, the densest of a large old village does, and plots grown close together
would: 1.1 at four plot households together, 1.5 to 1.63 at six and more (the second mock's arithmetic, not a measurement).

For each place the reports give `M / (d - b)` and `S / c` by decade, with which binds. In the mock neither binds a place before its
makers' shared custom does, but on a full island: there the farms' grain binds first (finding 20).

### 5.7 The work line, and what the band now means (answer 4)

Two lines are reported: **heads** (households by their relation to production: elite by VB-318's view when it exists; the rest by the
household's ladder), and **work**, in full-time equivalents of hours worked:

| Counted as | Hours |
| --- | --- |
| Farming | Fields, hay, herding and milking, fishing, hunting and gathering, and the carrying they need |
| Not farming | **All craft hours** (Kim): spinning and weaving, smelting and mending, pots, tar, brewing, a mill's or a kiln's work, whoever they are for; carrying goods to deal and the dealing; service and days worked for another household's craft |
| Household work, reported apart | Cooking, water, firewood, grinding at the quern, building and upkeep (Kim's choice 4: whether grinding and building count; the first mock read 403 with them, 239 without) |
| Elite | The hours of the members of an elite household (VB-318), by what they do |

**What the band means under answer 4.** When slice 6's goods exist, every farm household spends about 242 hours a year on dress, iron
and pots beside 769 on its food: 239 per 1,000. So the line no longer counts specialists. It reads **how much of the island's working
time is not spent getting food**, and 325 says "a third". The second mock's makers lift it to 263 to 275. To read 325 the island needs
about 128 more such hours for each farm household a year, half as much again as the 242: for instance 1.4 more lengths of cloth a
household (made for a feast, a dowry, a patron, an exchange), or the elite's building and service, or carrying by sea. And
the line **falls as work specialises**, since a master makes in 71 hours the cloth a novice makes in 153. So beside the line the report
gives the share of non-farm hours whose product left the household (the first mock's reading), which is the one that rises with
specialisation. The band proposed in the draft, 550 to 700 / 250 to 400 / 50 to 100 on the work line, is kept as a report that never
fails: under answer 4 its middle is met at the lower edge by the looms alone, and its upper half is what an elite and a ship add.

Today the line reads near 1,000 / 0 / 0: no cloth, iron or pots are made yet (fish drying and tar are a few points).

### 5.8 Left as a shape: service, lodging and the pull

The draft's 3.5 and 3.7 stand as written there and are not planned further: a head short of hands offers service (VB-694); a lodging is
a basis of the offer; "Marriage in town" is a custom; an adult weighs its place at home, what it has heard of the place, and the one
who offers. Each needs a place with households that are short of hands for their craft, which neither mock produced.
