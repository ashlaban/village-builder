# Review: growth, wealth and goals over a generation

25 November 2026. A review of how the design lets a pawn and a village grow, gather wealth and pursue plans over about twenty
years, a generation. It contrasts the design as it stands with the job system of the sister session (plans at four scales and the
levy, `docs/plans/four-scales/README.md`) and ranks where development should go. Asked for by Kim, on design only: no code is
read and no run is made. The focus is the individual pawn and the village; the household appears where a pawn's or a village's
goal passes through it.

Read for it: `design.md` as on `origin/claude/dreamy-allen-vhu47g` at `1efdf28f` (main at `4947938` plus the four-scales design),
sections 1 (Kim's terms, Principles), 4 (Marriage, nursing and growth; the twelve traits), 5 (Succession to Roads to the elite),
6 (Land and rights), 7 (Plans at four scales; The levy and own hours; How a holder chooses), 8 (side crafts; the mill and the

## 1. In short

- **The design is strong on what slows growth and weak on what lets a gain last.** Every brake has a named mechanism and
  timescale (the growth budget, the brakes of Roads to the elite, debtors' politics, the generational overshoot). Almost every
  soft gain halves in three or four years (goodwill, ale's standing, a story), food rots (about 22% of the grain harvest), houses
  and ways wear. Over twenty years a soft gain is earned again five or six times. Only land, animals, buildings and obligations
  carry a gain across a generation, and land cannot be bought.
- **No store of value lasts twenty years before slice 6's second half.** Grain rots by design ("rot is what stops hoarding"),
  goodwill decays, the herd is capped by hay and the stint, land is not sold, and silver and amber come with slice 6's second
  half and coin with slice 8. A pawn or a village that wants something ten years out has nothing to save in.
- **Plans stop at the year.** The plan record's highest horizon is the year. A village's routines are set afresh each year. A
  household's projects come from a menu when the year's targets are met. The one reckoning over several years in the whole
  design is the alderman's five-year test for a built way (section 2, Built ways). "Aim" is a slot in the plan record with no
  content: a pawn's aims wait for slice 8.
- **The levy creates a budget whose purpose waits for the last slice.** Own hours exist "for" the potter's road (Kim, 25
  November), and that road needs personal goods, which are slice 8's, and slice 8 now comes last (Kim, 16 November). Until then
  sovereignty is a measure with nothing to buy. Of the road's four preconditions, only personal goods are truly slice 8's: skill
  that grows is slice 6's design, and the plot for a landless maker is slice 9's.
- **The pawn has a life ladder but no wealth of its own.** The rungs (heir, marry onto a holding, empty farm, plot, new land,
  service) and the waits that hold marriage back are the design's best generational mechanism. Once a pawn is placed, its
  goals become its household's. A member owns nothing, its skill dies with it, and the servant's stake is the one thing a pawn
  saves.
- **The village grows in people but has no capital of its own.** What it builds (the mill, a way, a bell) is decided by a
  rule or a yearly vote, with no plan that spans years and no way to save toward one. Its gains go to the alderman's household.
  The town mocks found that "a place begun gathered comes apart in a generation": nothing makes a village's craft outlast the
  pawns who practise it.

**Priorities** (section 6): (1) a store of value that lasts a generation, and the ways into it; (2) personal goods and the
stake, pulled forward to the levy; (3) aims, one structure for every pawn and for the player, in the year list with a reserve;
(4) village projects that span years, paid for by levies; (5) craft and name that outlast a pawn; (6) land that can be bartered
and sold, at a high worth (Kim, 25 November); and, beside them all and first because it is cheap, (0) a generational report, so the
rest can be measured.

## 2. What a goal over a generation needs

A plan that pays off in ten or twenty years needs five things. Each section below reads the design against them.

| Need | What it means | Where the design should supply it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A stock that lasts** | Something held that does not decay away in less than the goal's span | Stores, land, animals, buildings, obligations, things of worth |
| **A conversion** | A way to turn this year's surplus into that stock, and the stock back into what the goal needs | Projects, exchange, loans, the gift, the fair |
| **A horizon** | A place in the plan where a commitment of years is held, with its progress and its deadline | The plan record (year, and above) |
| **A measure** | What the pawn and the player see of progress, by the pawn's estimate | Standing, class, sovereignty, the strip, the chronicle |
| **A brake** | What keeps a reinforcing gain from running away, on a named timescale | Decay, debtors' politics, inheritance, the cap |

The design has the fifth in abundance, the first and second for land and herds only, the third up to a year, and the fourth as
derived labels with hysteresis.

## 3. What carries over the years

### 3.1 The stocks

| Stock | Held by | How it grows | How it leaves | Lasts | Passes on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grain and food | Household | Harvest | Eaten, sown, feasted, given, lent; rots (`ECON.storeLoss`, about 22% of the harvest) | A year or two | With the farm |
| Land (fields, meadow) | Household (`own`, `use`) | Clearing while land is free; a claim; inheritance; foreclosure | Foreclosure; split between heirs; let go by the head's estimate | Indefinitely; soil wears to its floor | With the farm, by the custom's order |
| Animals | Household | Breeding | Slaughter; shares in animals; hunger | A beast's life; the herd indefinitely | With the farm; shares |
| Buildings and facilities | Household; village | Long work | Wear (a house 0.1 a year, 4 hours mend 0.1) | Decades if kept | With the farm |
| Goodwill | Pawn with pawn | Gifts, feasts, help | Halves in 4 years | ~4 years | Half to the new holder at handover |
| Standing | Pawn (derived) | Ale (3 years), house condition, office 0.3, elite 0.2 | Its sources fade | ~3 years, or while the source lasts | No (but the house passes) |
| Opinion, memories, stories | Pawn | Events | Half-life by wits | Years | No |
| Skill | Pawn | Hours at a craft (half in ~400 hours) | A tenth a year unused | A working life | No |
| The servant's stake | Pawn | 12 rations a year served | Spent on marriage or new land | Until spent | With the pawn |
| Obligations (loans, rents, shares, wages) | Body | Lending, letting, a case | Paid, forgiven, lapsed | Until met | To the successor |
| Things of worth (silver, amber) | Household | Slice 6 second half | Given, buried, lost | Do not wear | Dowry, inheritance |
| Office | Pawn | Election | Death, retirement, turns | The holder's tenure | No (an heir must win it) |
| Village store, commons, customs | Village | A quarter of surplus to the store's target; meeting votes | Help in hunger; attitudes move in decades | Store a year or two; customs decades | The village is permanent |

**The pattern.** The soft stocks, the ones a pawn's ambition works through (goodwill, standing, opinion), last three to four
years. The hard stocks that last a generation are land, animals, buildings and obligations. A gain lasts only where it can be
turned into a hard stock.

### 3.2 The ways from surplus into a lasting stock

| From surplus to | Mechanism | Open when | Verdict over 20 years |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Land | Clearing fields (a project: "more fields than needed"); a claim | While land is free within reach | Good early; closes when the land within a day's walk is taken |
| Land | Foreclosure through a loan, a default and a case | The debt road "opens only when the map is full (about year 150)" | Too late and too slow for a generation; by design |
| Land | Purchase | Never: "Land is not sold" (Proposed, 11 Nov) | Closed |
| Animals | Hay and breeding | Always, up to hay and the commons' stint | The one store of value open throughout; bounded by fodder |
| Buildings | Projects; long work | When the year's targets are met | Wear makes them a standing upkeep, not a saving |
| Goodwill, then standing | Feasts, good-year gifts | Grain above 2 years of need | Decays in 4 years: a treadmill, which is the design's intent |
| Office | Standing wins an election | At a vacancy | Ends with the holder |
| Claims on others | Grain loans at a quarter's interest | A neighbour's lean year | Due at one harvest; a store of value only through default |
| Things of worth | Slice 6 second half | Later | The first good that does not wear and needs no fodder |
| Personal stake | A servant's wage | Only in service | The one personal saving in the design |

**Between free land and foreclosure** there is no road from surplus into land. On populous that gap opens within the first
decades (adults waiting for land rose from 3 to 47 in 50 years on populous small); the debt road opens at about year 150. A
family that does well in that span can feast, keep beasts and mend its house, and pass on the same fields it inherited.

## 4. The pawn

### 4.1 What works

- **The life as rungs** (The round as offers). "Where do I live next year" asked each season, answered at the round, ranked by
  the pawn's wish and shifted by ambition, courting, a refusal and hunger. It is a one-year decision taken again each year, but
  its outcome (a holding) is the largest lasting stock a pawn ever gains.
- **The waits are modelled** (A wait is the thing waited for): a holding, the means (1.5 years of food), provisions for new
  land, years in service. The servant's portion (VB-637) is the design's one personal saving toward a goal, and it works: two
  wages save the provisions in about four years, the wait history shows. **It is the pattern to generalise** (priority 2).
- **Position decides fate, not hours** (The levy and own hours): heir, son, servant owe the same levy; position works through
  who stays, who serves, who inherits and what the head grants. A pawn's place in a lineage is its generational story.

### 4.2 What is missing

- **A placed pawn has no goals of its own.** Once a holder, its goals are its household's projects. As a member, it has own
  hours and, before slice 8, nothing that lasts to spend them on: pastimes, courting, rest, and hours given back.
- **Nothing a pawn makes is its own.** Personal goods wait for slice 8. So the head's grant of hours to a son, the act the levy
  adds, has no return for the son before then.
- **Skill does not outlast its holder.** It is learnt by doing and lost a tenth a year unused. Hands and husbandry pass as traits;
  skill is not taught. A household's craft lives as long as its practitioner.
- **Ambition has no object.** The ambition trait moves the wish to move out, the ale evening, and later pressing a debt and
  standing for office. Nothing names what an ambitious pawn wants, so nothing can show its progress or its failure.
- **The player's ambitions are postponed** (Kim, 7 November) "since nothing else depends on them". That has changed: the plan
  record now has an `aim` step and an `aim` verb, and Every act is a pawn's requires that whatever the played pawn has, every
  pawn has. A player's ambition and an unplayed pawn's aim must be the same structure. Designing aims designs ambitions.

### 4.3 Against the job system

The four-scales design supplies the grammar a generational goal needs and stops short of the content.

| The job system gives | It lacks, for a generation |
| --- | --- |
| One plan record with an author on each step; dated steps with `by`; steps at risk handed back to the default author | A horizon above the year, or year steps with a `by` several years out |
| The levy as a budget and own hours as its remainder; sovereignty as the measure | A use for own hours that accumulates before slice 8 |
| Grant, ask, demand and take as verbs on both sides | A lasting consequence: a breach costs goodwill only, which halves in 4 years. "Room is left" for standing, inheritance and a servant's term |
| `aim` as a step and a verb | Any `AimKey`, any weight from traits, any reserve |
| Booking of hours: given against taken | A link from a member's record of given and taken hours to the head's choice of heir and portion, which the README names and the design leaves for later |

**A generational dynamic it creates.** Giving replaces "work beats idling" with a pawn's diligence. Diligent members make
richer households; richer households marry their children sooner (the means); traits are partly inherited. Over two or three
generations diligence may be selected for. A culture's values are watched for exactly this (A culture's values, Watched). It
should be reported once the levy is built.

**A cost to growth it creates.** Realistic levies sit below the hours worked today (67 to 85% of the working day from spring to
autumn, against levies of 9.5 to 10.5 of 12). The difference is now the member's to give or keep. Unless members give most of
it, households have fewer hours, smaller surpluses and later marriages, so growth falls. Kim has said a measurement follows;
the growth target (The island grows without the player) is the line to watch.

## 5. The village

### 5.1 What works

- **Growth in people is well designed**: plots cleared by village work, daughter villages on new land within six hours' walk,
  fresh land's bonus, the growth budget's brakes and the wave a generation long.
- **The levy gives the village a budget**: a routine is adult-days in a window, split by the custom of lots, named by the
  household, with a ladder and a breach. This is the funding mechanism a long village project needs.
- **The alderman's five-year reckoning** for a built way is the right shape for village investment: a pawn's estimate of
  savings over years against building hours, put to the meeting.

### 5.2 What is missing

- **No village plan spans years.** Routines are set afresh each year, at most four. The mill is a rule ("a village of 3 farms or
  more builds a water mill"), not a decision with a cost reckoned and saved toward. It needs millstones and iron fittings,
  which may need trade first: a goal of several years with no place to hold it.
- **The village keeps no capital.** The store holds help for hunger to a target; the keep and the store's help go to the
  alderman in person. A village's lasting wealth is its customs, its commons and whatever facilities a rule built.
- **The office has no reason to invest.** Its gains are personal and end with the holder; the payers' pull leans against it.
  Kim's own picture ("an alderman can gather wealth and standing, collect a debt in land, pay for a mill, and become the
  first level of landed elite") needs a way from office to a lasting stock, and the only one designed is the debt road at year
  150.
- **Nothing locks a village's specialism in.** Goods of place make villages differ, and side crafts make "the fisher" and "the
  tar burner". The town mocks found makers spread and a gathered place comes apart in a generation. A facility passes with its
  household; the skill to use it does not.
- **A proposer has no stake in a project.** The new `propose` verb lets any holder put a routine on the agenda. Nothing gives the
  proposer standing when it is done or a cost when it fails, so ambition has no village object either.

## 6. Priorities

Ranked by how much generational play each opens for the pawn and the village, how much of it rests on pieces already designed or
built, and how far it is from the slices that block it. Each is a direction for a design pass, not a decided rule.

| # | Priority | What it opens | Rests on | Blocked by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | **A generational report** | Measuring everything below | The record, class, the bands | Nothing |
| 1 | **A store of value that lasts a generation** | Any goal past two years | Animals, obligations, things of worth | Slice 6b for silver |
| 2 | **Personal goods: the stake, generalised** | A use for own hours; the head's grant as investment | The servant's stake (VB-637); title moves at once | Nothing: pulled forward (Kim, Q2) |
| 3 | **Aims, one structure for pawn and player** | Goals the pawn holds and the player sees | `aim` in the plan record; rungs; projects; VB-1472 | Priorities 1 and 2 for most aims; un-postponed (Kim, Q3) |
| 4 | **Village projects across years** | A village agenda over a generation; a reason for the office | Routines as levies; `propose`; the five-year reckoning | Slice 6 for the mill |
| 5 | **Craft and name that outlast a pawn** | Specialist villages; a family's name | Skills (slice 6); stories (slice 7); the memorial stone | Slices 6 and 7 |
| 6 | **Land that can be bartered and sold, at a high worth** | Families rising before year 150; a store of value | Tenancy, shares, the worth table, the offer | A design pass (Kim, Q4: land is tradeable) |

### 0. A generational report (first, because it is cheap)

Report and never assert (An elite is what the rules leave over): for each cohort born in a decade, where its members stand at 40
by their parents' class (a mobility table); for households, durable wealth (land, beasts, buildings, things of worth, obligations
net) at each generation's handover, its spread, and how often a household's rank among its village changes in 20 years; for
villages, what was built and by whose proposal; for pawns, the share of own hours given, kept and taken by trait. It tells
whether the design today gives a 20-year arc at all, before any rule is changed. It belongs beside the slice bands
(`tests/lib/bands.js`).

### 1. A store of value that lasts a generation

The design keeps grain from piling up on purpose, and that should stand. What it lacks is a form a surplus can take that keeps
for a decade without a fodder cost. Options, in the order they rest on what exists:

- **The herd as the store of value**, with its brake named: hay and the commons' stint. History fits (cattle as wealth). It
  needs a head to aim for a herd above its need, and a stint that the meeting can move (debtors' politics over the stint).
- **Claims on others**: loans whose terms run longer than one harvest, a share owed for years, a portion owed by a household.
  The obligation record carries them already; only terms longer than a year are new.
- **Things of worth earlier**: amber on a shore and bog-iron goods are of place and need no ship. Pulling a first thing of
  worth into slice 6's first half gives a store that keeps and has a way out (What does not wear needs a way out).

**Guard**: a goal that saves needs an earmark. The feast's two years, the gift's allowance and the lender's spare all read
"above the floor". A reserve for an aim must be subtracted by them, as the seed is, or every saving is feasted.

### 2. Personal goods: the stake, generalised

The servant's stake is already title to goods in a named store, moving with its pawn (Title moves at once; custody moves by a
journey). Generalise it: what a pawn makes or earns in kept hours is booked to its stake. That is the one missing precondition
of the potter's road that is truly slice 8's, and it does not need the ship: it needs the levy (to say which hours are kept)
and a good to make. With it, a head's grant of hours to a son is an investment, a son's portion grows by his own work, and
sovereignty has something to buy. **Pull personal goods forward to the levy's arch, or to slice 6**, and leave coin with slice 8.
This revises the slice order Kim set, so it is a question (section 7, Q2).

### 3. Aims, one structure for pawn and player

Give `AimKey` a small set, each an object a pawn can hold for years, each read from the state (they grant nothing), each tied to
a rung or a project that exists:

| Aim | Progress read from | Brake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A holding of my own | Rungs open; stake against the means and provisions | The queue for land |
| A place for each child | Children placed (a farm, a plot, a marriage onto a holding) | Land; portions owed |
| A portion for a daughter | Stake or reserve against a dowry's worth | Feasting, hunger |
| A craft to live by | Skill; income from others against need | Material rights; the market's reach |
| The office | Standing against the last election's margin | Payers' pull; goodwill's decay |
| A house of standing | The house's grade and condition | Wear; materials of place |
| A herd | Beasts against the stint | Hay; the stint |

Each is held as a year step with a `by` years ahead and, where it saves, a reserve (priority 1's guard). Traits weigh which a
pawn takes (ambition, generosity, piety, honour), and its own estimate says whether it is on track, so the at-risk machinery of
the four scales already gives the decision point: lower the aim, demand more of members, ask a patron. For the player this is
the ambitions of the Vision, un-postponed by parity. VB-1472 (how a holder's parallel projects are limited) should be designed
with it, since most aims open projects.

### 4. Village projects across years

Let the meeting vote a project, not only a routine: a mill, a bridge, a common meadow, a bell, later a church. Its record is a
year step on the village's plan with a `by` and a levy laid each year until done, in adult-days by the custom of lots, with the
goods it needs as a target the village store or a trade must meet. Generalise the alderman's five-year reckoning so that any
holder may `propose` with an estimate, and book the result to the proposer: standing when it is done, a memory of the
village's when it is abandoned. This gives the office a road to something that lasts (a mill he paid for and its tolls to the
village) and ambition a village object, with the payers' pull as the brake already designed.

### 5. Craft and name that outlast a pawn

- **Skill learnt beside a master.** A pawn working a craft beside one more skilled learns faster. It is a mechanism, not a
  prescription, and it gives a household's craft a life past its practitioner: the son of the tar burner is the next tar
  burner because he worked beside him. It is the candidate the town mocks lacked for locking a specialism in at the scale of a
  generation (the facility, the elite buyer and the ship's buyer were tried and failed).
- **A family's name.** The memorial stone (Proposed) and farm names (*gårdsnamn*, an open proposal) are the design's only
  standing that belongs to a line. Stories of slice 7 could carry a house's repute across a generation, read by the match score
  and the vote. The player is the line; a line with no name of its own has nothing to show for twenty years.

### 6. Land that can be bartered and sold, at a high worth

Between free land and foreclosure a family that does well cannot turn surplus into land. Kim's answer (Q4): land is barterable
and tradeable, and dear; the design's statements otherwise were a stand-in to let development go on. With land tradeable,
land is also the store of value priority 1 looks for: it does not rot, needs no fodder and passes with the farm. Section 7, Q4,
says what a design pass must settle.

### Lower priority for this aim

Tuning the decays (goodwill's 4 years, ale's 3) would lengthen the soft stocks, but they are the brakes and work as designed;
the fix is a hard stock to convert into, not a slower decay. A horizon of "the generation" in the plan record is not needed:
year steps with a `by` years out carry it, and keep one record.

## 7. Questions for Kim, and the answers (25 November 2026)

1. **Which story should a generation tell: a house that rises over generations, or each life that rises and falls?**
   *Open:* Kim asked for it to be elaborated; section 8 does so and proposes an answer.
2. **May personal goods come forward from slice 8 to the levy's arch or to slice 6?** **Yes** (Kim). A pawn's stake, the
   servant's generalised, holds what it makes or earns in its kept hours; coin and silver stay with slice 8. What follows: the
   four-scales path's step 9 (own hours as a livelihood) loses its wait for slice 8 except for coin. The potter's road still
   needs skill that grows (slice 6), a market in reach (the gathering, the walk to a door) and a landless pawn's right to its
   material; the last is now the one precondition without a home, and goes to the design pass with the stake.
3. **May ambitions be un-postponed as pawns' aims**, one structure for every pawn by parity? **Yes** (Kim). What follows: the
   Vision's row Ambitions and section 16's open item change from postponed to designed; the first set is priority 3's table,
   designed with VB-1472, since most aims open projects; the player's ambitions are its pawn's aims, shown in the chronicle and
   on the strip's year zoom.
4. **Does "Land is not sold" stand?** **No.** Kim: land should be barterable and tradeable, though dear; any hard statement
   otherwise was a temporary measure to let development go on. Two rows say otherwise and fall: Land and rights, "Land is not
   sold" (Proposed, 11 Nov), and the fair's row on which exchanges are proper ("land is not offered at a fair at all, until
   slice 5's custom 'Debts are paid with' allows it"). `docs/review-markets.md` (5, "land is not on the table at a fair") is a
   review and is left as it was written.

   **What a design pass must settle.** Dear by mechanism, not by a price rule (Mechanisms, not prescriptions):
   - *Its worth.* A field's customary worth is `SHARES.perField` (120 rations) today, used for heirs' shares and the debt
     remedy. Land's worth should be years of what it yields its holder above the work it takes; the pass checks 120 against a
     field's net yield on the reference runs and says how many years of yield a field costs.
   - *Why a holder sells.* A judgement (A judgement is never a die): what it gets against what the land gives it, its heirs'
     places (a child loses its rung "heir to the farm at home"), the means to marry, and its class by land. A household sells
     mostly under need, as Hunger presses, the feast binds expects: land passes in dearth, as it does by debt today, but faster.
   - *The kin's right.* Medieval Swedish law gave the kin a first right to inherited land offered for sale, and a right to
     redeem it from an outsider (*bördsrätt*; the offer to kin, *lagbjudning*), and treated land a man had bought (*avlinge*)
     more freely than land he had inherited (*arv*). (From the standard literature, not checked in this session.) As a custom
     it keeps land in lines, differs between villages (Customs may prescribe) and is the brake on concentration that a market
     in land needs beside debtors' politics.
   - *A public act.* A sale is witnessed at the meeting, as a case is, so the village knows who holds what; title moves at once
     (Nothing teleports exempts rights).
   - *How it is paid.* Barter in goods of worth on the worth table (beasts, grain in whole quanta, later things of worth and
     coin), as one offer between two households (the offer's one shape); part may be owed as an obligation over years, which is
     priority 1's "claim that lasts".
   - *What it does to the balance.* Land concentration is one of the three balance properties Kim named first; a land market
     will move it sooner than the debt road (year 150). The slice band reports it, and the wrap-up names the brake that held.

Close calls in this review: placing the report first (it delays design passes by a little, and every later number rests on it);
and preferring the herd over earlier silver as the first store of value (silver has the cleaner brake, the herd is there now).
With land tradeable the second is less pressing: land becomes the main store of value, the herd the one a landless pawn can hold.

## 8. A house or a life: question 1, elaborated

### 8.1 Why the answer matters

A run of 40 to 80 hours covers about 230 years (The growth budget, The pace). Holders hand on at the heir's marriage or at 60,
men first marry at 22 to 23, so a generation, holder to heir, is about 30 years: a run holds seven or eight of them. The
player is the line (Vision, Player role): when its pawn dies it goes on as the successor. So the question is what the player
inherits seven times over: a position that its earlier lives built, or a fresh start near the village's middle.

The Vision already leans one way: "Social hierarchy grows from a few founding homesteads". A hierarchy can only grow from
homesteads if some of what a homestead gains outlasts its holder. If everything resets at each handover, the hierarchy is
re-drawn each generation and never grows.

### 8.2 The handover is the valve

Everything that crosses a generation crosses at one event, the handover (in life, or at the holder's death). What passes today,
and what brakes it:

| What | Passes to the heir? | The brake at the handover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The farm: house, fields, meadow | Whole, to one heir, under the default "one heir buys out the rest" | The siblings' shares: `SHARES.perField` a field plus the store's grain, owed by the farm and paid at each round from grain above its reserve. The richer the farm and the more children, the more it owes, for years. A brake that grows with wealth |
| The farm, under "split between heirs" | Divided | Partible inheritance cut estates and pauperised in the balance review's mock |
| Animals, buildings, facilities | With the farm | Shares may be paid in animals; buildings wear |
| Obligations owed and held | To the successor | None but their terms |
| Goodwill | Half to the new holder | Halves in 4 years |
| Standing, office, skill | No | The heir must earn them again |
| The head's ability | Traits partly inherited (wits, husbandry); a head's foresight drawn at becoming head | Regression toward the culture's mean: a poor heir loses ground |
| A daughter's dowry (slice 4's terms) | Out of the house, to her husband's | Counted against her share |

So the design already keeps the farm whole and brakes its wealth at the handover in proportion to it. What it lacks is anything
for the farm to gain beyond its fields, and anything of its holder's position to hand on. In Nordic terms the house that
persists is the farm (*gården*), not the family: the heir keeps the farm, and the younger children fall to service, a plot or a
cottage. That is already how the design works, and the player, following the heir, rides the farm's line.

### 8.3 Three answers

| | A. Each life rises and falls | B. A house rises over generations, held back each generation | C. A house rises, held back only over centuries |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The measure**: how closely an heir's wealth rank follows its parent's at the same age | low (about 0 to 0.3) | middling (about 0.4 to 0.6) | high (0.7 and above) |
| **What passes** | The farm as today; little else exists to pass | The farm, and what it gained: land bought or cleared, the herd, things of worth, claims; a name | All of B, and the position: the office to the heir, rank, an entailed estate |
| **What brakes it** | Decay of every soft stock; the office ending | The siblings' shares and dowries, growing with wealth; poor heirs; dearth forcing sales; the kin's right; debtors' politics | Only what slice 8 brings: rank from outside, the Crown, war |
| **What the player lives** | Each life a fresh climb from the middle; the line's standing flat with spikes; a bad life costs nothing later | Two or three generations to rise; one bad heir or a run of dearth can undo it; the chronicle shows the house's curve | A dynasty: the first generations decide the rest |
| **History** | A frontier in its first generations | The Swedish free farmers: farms kept in a line for centuries, a few lines of *storbönder* rising by land and marriage, and from 1280 (the Alsnö ordinance) tax freedom for one who could furnish a horseman, a road to the elite by wealth | The nobility once closed, from the 16th and 17th centuries |
| **Risk** | Twenty years show nothing; ambition has no object past one life | A run-away house if the brakes are weak, which the report shows | The elite closes, against An elite is what the rules leave over |

(The historical column is from the standard literature and not checked in this session. The ranges of the measure are
indicative: estimates for pre-industrial societies vary widely, and some long-run studies of surnames find status persisting at
0.7 or more over centuries. They are a scale for the report, not targets.)

### 8.4 Proposed: B

A house may rise over two or three generations and lose it in one, as the free farmers' houses did.

- **It is what the Vision asks** ("hierarchy grows from a few founding homesteads") and what the default custom already does
  (the farm kept whole, the siblings' shares as a brake that grows with wealth).
- **It gives the twenty-year arc its stakes.** An aim of a holder (priority 3: a place for each child, land, the office) is
  partly for its heir; a choice at year 30 is felt at year 60.
- **It needs little new.** What passes is the hard stocks; the work is giving the farm hard stocks to gain: land by sale and
  barter (Q4), a herd and things of worth (priority 1), and a name (priority 5). The soft stocks keep their decay.
- **Its brakes exist and should be checked, not invented:** do the siblings' shares grow with all the farm's wealth, not only
  its fields and grain (beasts, things of worth, land bought)? Does a dowry leave the house in proportion to it? Does the
  kin's right on a sale send land back along lines? The design pass answers these.
- **The office stays a life's** (A's rule for it): a son who follows his father as alderman is one of the ways of choosing
  that Kim designed on 15 November, a practice a village may hold, not a rule. Rank that passes is slice 8's (C's road), and
  stays there.

**What it would change in the design, if accepted:** a row in Vision and scope (what a generation is about); in Handover and
shares, the shares counted on all of a farm's wealth; in the report (priority 0), the measure of 8.3 by village and by culture,
beside the elite band, with no target; in the chronicle, a house's curve over its holders.

**Deciding it:** if B, priorities 1, 5 and 6 are the house's, and priority 0's measure says whether the brakes hold. If A,
priorities 1 and 6 still matter for a single life, and 5 drops. If C, rank and estates come forward from slice 8, which
reopens the slice order.
